# Ancient engineering

**Type:** lesson

**Ancient engineering** is the work of early civilizations that shaped water, stone, and earth into cities and monuments using simple tools. Engineers today should know it because those builders faced the same trade-offs of materials, labor, water, and upkeep that shape every structure built now. Engineers in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, Greece, Rome, China, Persia, India, and the Andes built cities, water systems, roads, and monuments with levers, ramps, sledges, gravity, and local stone.

**Prerequisites:** [Simple machines](01-simple-machines.md), [Algebra](../math/01-algebra.md), [Control volumes and pipe flow](../physics/11-control-volumes-pipe-flow.md)
**Time:** 4-5 h

## Core ideas

Early engineering began with water. Farmers in the river valleys needed to move water to fields and to keep floods away from homes. Cities grew once farmers could feed more people, and cities needed walls, temples, roads, and drains. Each civilization solved these problems with the tools and materials it had, and it passed on the methods it had tested.

### Rivers and early cities, about 3500 to 2000 BCE

Sumerian city-states in Mesopotamia, between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, dug irrigation canals and built dikes. The earliest wheels in the region date to about 3500 BCE. Farmers lifted water with the **shaduf**, a counterweighted lever, which Mesopotamia and Egypt both used. Mudbrick and baked brick built temple towers, including the ziggurat at Ur, which dates to about 2100 BCE.

Egypt depended on the yearly Nile flood. Basin irrigation spread the water across fields. Egyptian builders moved from mudbrick to stone. The step pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara, dating to about 2650 BCE, is credited to the architect Imhotep and is among the first large stone monuments. The Great Pyramid of Giza, built for Khufu about 2560 BCE, uses more than two million stone blocks. Crews hauled blocks on sledges, wetted the sand ahead of the sledges to cut friction, and used ramps of stone and mudbrick to raise the blocks. The workers' settlements found at Giza point to organized, paid labor rather than slavery.

In the Indus Valley, the cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro were built between about 2600 and 1900 BCE. Their streets ran on a grid, their houses used standard baked bricks, and covered drains carried waste away from the houses. The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro shows careful waterproofing with bitumen.

### Tunnels, aqueducts, and irrigation, about 1000 BCE to 250 BCE

Persian engineers built **qanats**, gently sloping underground channels fed by vertical shafts. Gravity moves the water, so no pump is needed. Qanats appear across Iran and still supply water in some places. Their exact start date is uncertain, but many are more than two thousand years old.

On the island of Samos, the engineer Eupalinos of Megara dug a tunnel of about one kilometre through a mountain around 530 BCE. Crews dug from both ends and met near the middle. The tunnel carried water to the city of Samos. Herodotus described the tunnel, which makes it one of the earliest engineering works with a written account.

Archimedes worked in Syracuse in the third century BCE. He described the law of the lever, the compound pulley, and a screw for raising water, which is traditionally credited to him. The [simple machines](01-simple-machines.md) lesson covers these machines in detail.

In Sichuan, China, the Dujiangyan irrigation system dates to about 256 BCE. A stone divider splits the Min River into an inner and an outer channel. The inner channel feeds irrigation, and the outer channel carries floodwater away. The system is traditionally credited to Li Bing and his son, and it still irrigates farmland today.

In Rome, the first aqueduct, the Aqua Appia, opened in 312 BCE under Appius Claudius Caecus. Most Roman aqueducts carried water in channels that fell gently under the pull of gravity. Where a valley had to be crossed, builders used **inverted siphons**, which push water up the far slope with pressure in closed pipes.

### Concrete, bridges, and monuments, about 50 CE to 610 CE

Roman builders mixed lime, volcanic ash called **pozzolana**, and rubble to make concrete. The ash reacts with lime and water to form a hard, durable material. The Pont du Gard in southern France, about 50 CE, carries an aqueduct across a river valley on three tiers of arches. The Pantheon in Rome, built under Hadrian about 125 CE, has a concrete dome about 43 metres across with no steel reinforcement. Roman roads used layered stone bases to spread loads and to shed water.

In India, the Iron Pillar of Delhi dates to about 400 CE. Its wrought iron resists rust far better than most iron of its time, and it has stood for more than 1500 years with little corrosion. Stepwells in western India stored water and let people reach it at many levels.

In China, the Grand Canal linked river systems in the Sui dynasty between about 605 and 610 CE. It carried grain and goods between the south and the north. Parts of the canal are still in use.

### The Americas, 1200 to 1500 CE

The Inca built a road network of thousands of kilometres across the Andes. Bridges spanned deep gorges, and some were woven from grass rope. The Q'eswachaka bridge is still rebuilt every year by local communities using the same method. Inca builders also cut terraces into mountain slopes to farm steep ground and to control water. In the Maya lowlands, cities built raised causeways called sacbeob, and they stored rainwater in reservoirs and cisterns because surface water is scarce in much of the lowlands.

## Key equations

Flow in an open channel, such as an aqueduct, follows the Manning equation in SI units:

$$V = \frac{1}{n} R_h^{2/3} S^{1/2}$$

Here $V$ is the mean velocity in metres per second (m/s), $n$ is the **Manning roughness coefficient** (units $\text{s/m}^{1/3}$, typical value 0.013 for smooth stone or mortar lining), $R_h$ is the hydraulic radius in metres (m), and $S$ is the channel slope, a ratio of drop to length with no units. The **hydraulic radius** is the flow area $A$ divided by the wetted perimeter $P$:

$$R_h = \frac{A}{P}$$

The flow rate follows from continuity, with $A$ in square metres ($\text{m}^2$):

$$Q = A V$$

Here $Q$ is the flow rate in cubic metres per second ($\text{m}^3$/s).

## Worked example

**Given:** A stone aqueduct channel is $0.60\ \text{m}$ wide with water $0.80\ \text{m}$ deep. The channel falls $1.0\ \text{m}$ in every $1000\ \text{m}$, so $S = 0.001$. The Manning coefficient is $n = 0.013$, a typical value for a smooth stone or mortar lining. The channel size and slope are assumed for this exercise, not taken from a specific Roman aqueduct.

**Find:** (a) the mean velocity, (b) the flow rate in litres per second and cubic metres per day, and (c) the time for water to travel 1 km.

**Assumptions:** Steady flow in a uniform channel. Gravity alone drives the flow.

**Solution:**

1. Flow area: $A = 0.60 \times 0.80 = 0.48\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Wetted perimeter: $P = 0.60 + 2 \times 0.80 = 2.20\ \text{m}$.
3. Hydraulic radius: $R_h = A / P = 0.48 / 2.20 = 0.218\ \text{m}$.
4. Velocity: $V = (1 / 0.013) \times 0.218^{2/3} \times 0.001^{1/2} = 0.882\ \text{m/s}$.
5. Flow rate: $Q = 0.48 \times 0.882 = 0.423\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$, which is $423\ \text{L/s}$.
6. Daily volume: $0.423 \times 86\,400 = 36\,600\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}$, rounded from 36 561.
7. Travel time for 1 km: $1000 / 0.882 = 1134\ \text{s}$, which is 18.9 min.

**Answer:** (a) The mean velocity is 0.88 m/s. (b) The flow is 0.42 $\text{m}^3$/s, or 423 L/s, which is about 36 600 $\text{m}^3$ per day. (c) Water takes about 19 minutes to travel 1 km.

**Sanity check:** Velocity grows with the square root of slope, so halving the slope to 1 in 2000 should cut velocity by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$. Recomputing gives $V = 0.623\ \text{m/s}$, and $0.882 / 0.623 = 1.42$, which matches $\sqrt{2}$. Flow at the gentler slope is $0.299\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$. A single channel of this size moves about 36 600 $\text{m}^3$ of water each day.

## Lessons for engineers today

Gravity still moves more water than pumps do, and the Roman and Persian systems show why. A gentle, steady fall needs no power and only maintenance. Builders also chose materials for durability, and Roman harbor concrete still stands after nearly two thousand years. Finally, the Inca rope bridge and the Dujiangyan system survive because local people keep rebuilding and managing them, which is a lesson in maintenance planning that engineers still need.

## Practice

1. A lever lifts a 400 N load. The load arm is 0.5 m and the effort arm is 2.0 m. Ideally, what effort lifts the load?
2. Put these in order from oldest to newest: the Dujiangyan irrigation system, the Great Pyramid of Giza, the Aqua Appia, and the Eupalinos tunnel on Samos.
3. A channel has the same size as the worked example, but its slope is 1 in 2000. Find the flow rate in cubic metres per second using $n = 0.013$.
4. Explain why most Roman aqueducts used gravity rather than pumps, and say when a builder needed an inverted siphon instead.
5. Explain why the lime and volcanic ash in Roman concrete made the material durable.

2. Great Pyramid of Giza (about 2560 BCE), Eupalinos tunnel on Samos (about 530 BCE), Aqua Appia (312 BCE), Dujiangyan (about 256 BCE).
3. $V = 0.623\ \text{m/s}$ and $A = 0.48\ \text{m}^2$, so $Q = 0.299\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$.

## Common mistakes

- Saying the Romans invented the arch. Mesopotamian and Egyptian builders used arches before the Romans, and the Romans built with arches on a very large scale.
- Saying the pyramids were built by enslaved people. Evidence from the Giza workers' settlements points to organized, paid labor.
- Saying the Great Wall of China is visible from space with the naked eye. It is not, from orbit with unaided eyes.
- Saying Roman aqueducts carried water under pressure throughout. Most ran by gravity, and pressure pipes appeared only at valley crossings.
- Saying Roman concrete was reinforced with steel. The Pantheon dome is unreinforced concrete.
