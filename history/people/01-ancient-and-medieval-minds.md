# Ancient and medieval minds

**Type:** lesson

From about 2650 BCE to the early 1200s, people in Egypt, Greece, Rome, China, India, and the Islamic world built the first tools of engineering: geometry, mechanics, optics, and precise timekeeping. Their methods, from proofs to measured instruments, set the pattern that engineers still follow. This lesson profiles the people behind those tools and shows where their work survives today.

**Prerequisites:** [Simple machines](../01-simple-machines.md), [Hydrostatics and Bernoulli](../../physics/10-hydrostatics-and-bernoulli.md)
**Time:** 6-8 h

## Core ideas

The profiles run in rough time order. Each one gives when and where the person lived, what they built or discovered, and why it still matters. Dates marked "about" are approximate.

### Imhotep, about 2650 BCE, Saqqara, Egypt

Imhotep served King Djoser of Egypt as an official and architect. Tradition credits him with the Step Pyramid at Saqqara. It is one of the earliest large stone structures in the world, built as a stack of shrinking platforms. The project shows that large construction needs a planner who can organize labor, stone, and materials at the same time. Engineers still plan large builds the same way.

### Euclid, about 325 to 265 BCE, Alexandria, Egypt

Euclid taught mathematics in Alexandria. His *Elements* starts from a few definitions and postulates and proves the rest of plane and solid geometry step by step. The book was the standard geometry text for about two thousand years. Every construction drawing and every structural calculation rests on the kind of reasoning Euclid set out.

### Archimedes, 287 to 212 BCE, Syracuse, Sicily

Archimedes worked in Syracuse, a Greek city on Sicily. He studied the lever, the pulley, and the screw, and he explained why a body in a fluid loses weight equal to the fluid it pushes aside. This is Archimedes' principle. He also used regular polygons with 96 sides to bound $\pi$ between $3\tfrac{10}{71}$ and $3\tfrac{1}{7}$. Ship designers, hydrometer makers, and pump engineers still use the buoyancy principle, and the lever and screw are basic machines in every hydraulic and mechanical system.

### Hero of Alexandria, 1st century CE, Alexandria, Egypt

Hero wrote books on mechanics and pneumatics. His *Pneumatica* describes a sphere that spins when steam jets out of its nozzles, the aeolipile. It also describes devices that use water, air, and weights to open temple doors and dispense holy water. The books show that Greek engineers had already built working steam and pressure devices, even though those devices were not used for industry. Hero's work is an early example of a design that uses fluid pressure to do a job.

### Vitruvius, active in the late 1st century BCE, Rome

Vitruvius was a Roman military engineer and architect. His *De architectura* is a ten-book guide to building, covering materials, temples, city water supply, machines, and water-raising devices. It is the oldest surviving treatise on architecture from the Roman world. Renaissance architects read it closely, and its advice on proportion and water supply still appears in design codes and history courses.

### Zhang Heng, 78 to 139 CE, Eastern Han China

Zhang Heng was an astronomer, mathematician, and inventor in the Eastern Han dynasty. In 132 CE he built a seismoscope, a bronze vessel with eight dragon heads holding balls. When an earthquake struck, a ball fell from the dragon facing the direction of the quake. The original was lost, but modern replicas show the mechanism. Zhang Heng also built a water-driven armillary sphere that tracked the sky. His devices show early use of precise instruments to measure natural events.

### Brahmagupta, about 598 to about 670, India

Brahmagupta worked as an astronomer and mathematician in northwest India. His text *Brahmasphutasiddhanta*, written in 628, gives some of the earliest surviving rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers. Calculators, computer arithmetic, and every spreadsheet follow rules that trace back to his work. He also wrote on solving equations and on the geometry of triangles.

### Al-Khwarizmi, about 780 to about 850, Baghdad

Al-Khwarizmi worked at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad under the Abbasid caliph al-Mamun. His book on al-jabr and al-muqabala gave a systematic method for solving linear and quadratic equations. The Latin version of his name gave us the word algorithm, and his book gave us the word algebra. Every calculation an engineer writes as a step-by-step procedure is a descendant of his method.

### Ibn al-Haytham, about 965 to about 1040, Basra and Cairo

Ibn al-Haytham studied light through experiment. His *Book of Optics* explains that vision happens when light enters the eye, and it describes how a small hole in a dark room projects an inverted image. This idea underlies the camera obscura and the modern camera. He tested his claims with controlled observations, which made optics a science built on measurement.

### Al-Jazari, 1136 to about 1206, Diyarbakir, Anatolia

Al-Jazari served the Artuqid rulers as an engineer and built water-powered machines for them. His *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, completed in 1206, describes water clocks, a musical automaton, and pumps. The book shows crank and cam mechanisms that turn continuous rotation into timed motion. These mechanisms appear in engines, cam-driven valves, and automated machines today.

### Su Song, 1020 to 1101, Kaifeng, Song dynasty China

Su Song led the team that built an astronomical clock tower in Kaifeng, begun in 1088 and finished in the early 1090s. The tower used a water wheel to turn a model of the heavens and to strike bells on the hour. It used an escapement and a chain drive to control the motion. The tower is gone, but Su Song's book describes its design. The escapement idea later appears in mechanical clocks.

## Key equations

Archimedes' principle says a body in a fluid feels an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces:

$$F_b = \rho_f \, g \, V_{\text{sub}}$$

where $F_b$ is the buoyant force in newtons (N), $\rho_f$ is the fluid density in kilograms per cubic metre (kg/m$^3$), $g$ is the local gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s$^2$ at Earth's surface), and $V_{\text{sub}}$ is the volume of fluid displaced in cubic metres (m$^3$).

A floating body sinks until the displaced fluid weighs as much as the body. The fraction of the body underwater is:

$$\frac{V_{\text{sub}}}{V} = \frac{\rho_o}{\rho_f}$$

where $V$ is the body's total volume in m$^3$ and $\rho_o$ is the body's density in kg/m$^3$. Typical values are $\rho_f = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$ for fresh water and $\rho_o = 2700\ \text{kg/m}^3$ for aluminium.

## Worked example

### Given

A solid aluminium block has a mass of 1.00 kg and a density of 2700 kg/m$^3$. It hangs from a spring scale and is fully submerged in fresh water at 1000 kg/m$^3$.

### Find

The buoyant force, the block's true weight, and the reading on the spring scale.

### Assumptions

The block is fully submerged. The water is at rest and at room temperature. Water density is 1000 kg/m$^3$ and $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ (Earth surface).

### Solution

The block volume is the mass divided by the density:

$$V = \frac{m}{\rho_o} = \frac{1.00\ \text{kg}}{2700\ \text{kg/m}^3} = 3.70 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3 \quad (\text{370 cm}^3)$$

The buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced water:

$$F_b = \rho_f g V = (1000)(9.81)(3.70 \times 10^{-4}) = 3.63\ \text{N}$$

The true weight is:

$$W = m g = (1.00)(9.81) = 9.81\ \text{N}$$

The spring scale reads the weight minus the buoyant force:

$$W_{\text{app}} = W - F_b = 9.81 - 3.63 = 6.18\ \text{N}$$

### Answer

The buoyant force is 3.63 N. The true weight is 9.81 N. The spring scale reads 6.18 N.

### Sanity check

Aluminium is 2.7 times as dense as water, so the buoyant force should be 1 divided by 2.7 of the weight. That gives $9.81 / 2.7 = 3.63\ \text{N}$, which matches.

## Lessons for engineers today

Archimedes' principle is still the first tool for buoyancy, from ship hulls to submarine ballast and hydrometers that measure fluid density. The early engineers also show a habit that still applies: they tested ideas with physical devices and measured results. Vitruvius and al-Jazari wrote down designs so that others could build them, and that habit underlies every drawing and standard in use today. Brahmagupta's and al-Khwarizmi's rules show that a clear procedure, written once, can be used by anyone.

## Practice

1. Short answer. A steel ship floats even though steel is denser than water. Why? Explain in terms of average density and displaced water.

2. Calculation. A solid 4.0 kg block with density 8000 kg/m$^3$ is fully submerged in fresh water. Find the volume, the buoyant force, the true weight, and the apparent weight. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ (Earth surface).

3. Calculation. A wooden block with density 600 kg/m$^3$ and volume 0.020 m$^3$ floats in fresh water. Find the fraction submerged, the submerged volume, and the block's weight. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ (Earth surface).

4. Calculation. Archimedes bounded $\pi$ between $3\tfrac{10}{71}$ and $3\tfrac{1}{7}$. Find the two decimal values and the gap between them.

5. Short answer. Describe how the rotation of Archimedes' screw lifts water. Name the machine it belongs to in the simple machine list.

## Common mistakes

- The bath story. Vitruvius reports that Archimedes discovered buoyancy in a bath and ran naked through the city. That story comes from a Roman writer centuries later. It does not appear in Archimedes' surviving works.
- Buoyancy depends on the weight of the displaced fluid, not on the weight of the object. A heavy steel hull floats if it displaces enough water.
- Imhotep designed the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, not the pyramids at Giza. Those came about a century later and used different methods.
- Brahmagupta is often said to have invented zero. Zero was in use before him in several cultures. His contribution is the earliest surviving full set of rules for arithmetic with it.
- Hero's devices were not factory machines. They were demonstrations and temple mechanisms, so they show what was possible, not what was common.
