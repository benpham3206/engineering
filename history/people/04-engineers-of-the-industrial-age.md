# Engineers of the industrial age

**Type:** lesson

Engineers of the industrial age are the people who, from about 1750 to 1900, turned science and craft into machines, bridges, railways, and steel on a scale no earlier society had reached. Engineers today should know them because the machines, bridges, and steel they built set methods that modern engineers still use. This lesson covers thirteen people, from John Smeaton to Gustave Eiffel. Each entry says when and where the person worked, what they built, and which lesson in this repo uses that work.

**Prerequisites:** [Industrial revolution](../05-industrial-revolution.md), [Thermodynamics: entropy, the second law, and cycles](../../physics/09-thermo-second-law-cycles.md)
**Time:** 3-5 h

## Core ideas

### John Smeaton, 1724 to 1792

Smeaton lived and worked in England, mostly in Yorkshire and London. He rebuilt the Eddystone lighthouse on a rock off Cornwall between 1756 and 1759. For the mortar, he used a lime that sets under water, which made the stone tower last. Smeaton is often called the first man to describe himself as a civil engineer. Engineers use hydraulic lime and concrete in harbours, dams, and foundations. See [Phase diagrams and heat treatment](../../materials/04-phase-diagrams-heat-treatment.md) and [Bridges](../../applications/structures/01-bridges.md).

### James Watt, 1736 to 1819

Watt was born in Greenock, Scotland, and worked as an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow. In 1769 he patented a separate condenser for the steam engine. The condenser kept the cylinder hot on every stroke, which cut the steam and fuel the engine needed compared with Newcomen's engine. Watt later worked with Matthew Boulton in Birmingham to build and sell engines. The horsepower unit came from Watt's effort to compare his engines with horses. Engineers use the separate-condenser idea in every steam plant, and the horsepower unit is still used for motors and engines. See [Steam power plants](../../applications/thermal-fluid/08-steam-power-plants.md) and [Thermodynamics: second law and cycles](../../physics/09-thermo-second-law-cycles.md).

### Henry Maudslay, 1771 to 1831

Maudslay was born in Woolwich, near London, and worked in London workshops. He built a precise screw-cutting lathe with a lead screw, which allowed machines to make threads of a known pitch. Precise lead screws let a machine tool move a cutting tool by a known distance. Engineers use lead screws in every CNC mill, lathe, and linear actuator. See [Lead screws and ball screws](../../applications/power-transmission/08-lead-screws-and-ball-screws.md) and [CNC machines](../../applications/machines-robots/03-cnc-machines.md).

### Thomas Telford, 1757 to 1834

Telford was born in Westerkirk, Scotland, and worked as a bridge and road engineer across Britain. He completed the Pontcysyllte Aqueduct in Wales in 1805, which carries a canal on an iron trough above a river valley. He also completed the Menai Suspension Bridge, which opened in 1826. Engineers use the suspension and aqueduct designs he built as models for long spans and canal crossings. See [Bridges](../../applications/structures/01-bridges.md) and [Canals and railways](../projects/02-canals-and-railways.md).

### George Stephenson, 1781 to 1848

Stephenson was born in Wylam, Northumberland, in England. He built the locomotive *Locomotion* for the Stockton and Darlington Railway, which opened in 1825. He and his son Robert designed the *Rocket*, which won the Rainhill Trials in 1829. The *Rocket* showed that a light, fast steam locomotive could pull trains on a public line. Engineers use his railway standards, including the 4 ft $8\tfrac{1}{2}$ in gauge, in many of today's railways. See [Trains](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/10-trains.md).

### Robert Stephenson, 1803 to 1859

Robert was born in Willington, England, and worked with his father on early railways. He designed the *Rocket* with his father, and Robert Stephenson and Company built it. His father was chief engineer of the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, which opened in 1830. He later built the Britannia Bridge over the Menai Strait, completed in 1850, which used wrought-iron tubes to carry trains. Engineers still use box and tubular girders for long spans that carry trains. See [Bridges](../../applications/structures/01-bridges.md) and [Trains](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/10-trains.md).

### Isambard Kingdom Brunel, 1806 to 1859

Brunel was born in Portsmouth, England, and worked mostly in Bristol and London. He built the Great Western Railway, and his ships included the SS *Great Britain*, launched in 1843 with an iron hull and a screw propeller. He designed the Clifton Suspension Bridge, which opened in 1864, five years after his death. Engineers use his combined ship and rail designs as early examples of large iron structures in service. See [Ships and submarines](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/09-ships-and-submarines.md) and [Bridges](../../applications/structures/01-bridges.md).

### Joseph Whitworth, 1803 to 1887

Whitworth was born in Stockport, England, and worked as a machine tool maker. In 1841 he proposed a standard for screw threads, with a 55 degree thread angle and a set pitch for each diameter. The standard let different makers produce nuts and bolts that fit each other. Engineers use the Whitworth thread in older British machines and still use the same idea in every fastener standard. See [Gears, fasteners, and springs](../../design/09-gears-fasteners-and-springs.md) and [Tolerancing and stack-up](../../design/04-tolerancing-and-stack-up.md).

### Henry Bessemer, 1813 to 1898

Bessemer was born in Hertfordshire, England, and worked as an inventor in London. In 1856 he patented a process that blows air through molten pig iron. The air burns off the excess carbon, which made steel cheap and in large quantities. The original process could not remove phosphorus, so it worked only on low-phosphorus ores until Sidney Gilchrist Thomas solved the problem in 1879. Engineers use the principle of oxygen blowing in basic oxygen steelmaking, which replaced the Bessemer converter. See [Phase diagrams and heat treatment](../../materials/04-phase-diagrams-heat-treatment.md) and [Material classes](../../materials/05-material-classes.md).

### Sadi Carnot, 1796 to 1832

Carnot was born and died in Paris. In 1824 he published *Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire*. The book shows that the best possible heat engine depends only on the temperatures of its hot and cold reservoirs, and not on the working fluid. That gives the upper limit, $\eta = 1 - T_C / T_H$. Engineers use the Carnot limit to judge how close a real power plant or refrigerator comes to its best possible performance. See [Thermodynamics: second law and cycles](../../physics/09-thermo-second-law-cycles.md).

### John Augustus Roebling, 1806 to 1869

Roebling was born in Mühlhausen, Prussia, and emigrated to the United States. He made wire rope in Pennsylvania in the 1840s, which was stronger and more flexible than hemp rope. He designed the Brooklyn Bridge in New York. He died in 1869 from an injury at the bridge site, before construction was well underway. Engineers use his wire cable methods in suspension bridges and cable lifts. See [Towers and cables](../../applications/structures/06-towers-and-cables.md) and [Bridges](../../applications/structures/01-bridges.md).

### Emily Warren Roebling, 1843 to 1903

Emily Warren Roebling was born in Cold Spring, New York. Her husband, Washington Roebling, took over as chief engineer after his father's death. After he was disabled by caisson disease, she carried his instructions to the workers and supervised work at the site from about 1872. The Brooklyn Bridge opened on May 24, 1883. See [Bridges](../../applications/structures/01-bridges.md) and [Towers and cables](../../applications/structures/06-towers-and-cables.md). The bridge's history is also covered in [Bridges, dams, and towers](../projects/03-bridges-dams-and-towers.md).

### Gustave Eiffel, 1832 to 1923

Eiffel was born in Dijon, France, and ran a large iron-structures firm near Paris. His firm built the Maria Pia Bridge in Porto, Portugal, in 1877 and the Garabit Viaduct in France in 1884. The Eiffel Tower was completed in March 1889 for the Exposition Universelle in Paris. Its design came from Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier in Eiffel's office. Engineers use lattice towers and wind-load design in radio masts, transmission towers, and tall structures. See [Towers and cables](../../applications/structures/06-towers-and-cables.md) and [Bridges, dams, and towers](../projects/03-bridges-dams-and-towers.md).

## Key equations

Carnot efficiency for a reversible heat engine:

$$\eta = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}$$

where $\eta$ is efficiency (dimensionless), $T_H$ is the hot reservoir temperature in kelvin (K), and $T_C$ is the cold reservoir temperature in kelvin (K). Both temperatures must be absolute.

Mechanical horsepower, the unit named after Watt's engines:

$$1\ \text{hp} = 745.7\ \text{W}$$

where 1 hp is the mechanical horsepower and 745.7 W is its value in watts.

## Worked example

**Given:** A reversible heat engine takes heat from a hot reservoir at 600 °C and rejects heat to a cold reservoir at 30 °C. It must deliver 1.00 MJ of work.

**Find:** (a) the maximum efficiency, and (b) the heat taken from the hot reservoir and the heat rejected to the cold reservoir.

**Solution:**

1. Convert to kelvin: $T_H = 600 + 273.15 = 873.15\ \text{K}$, and $T_C = 30 + 273.15 = 303.15\ \text{K}$.
2. Efficiency: $\eta = 1 - 303.15 / 873.15 = 0.653$, which is 65.3 percent.
3. Heat from the hot reservoir: $Q_H = W / \eta = 1.00\ \text{MJ} / 0.653 = 1.53\ \text{MJ}$.
4. Heat rejected: $Q_C = Q_H - W = 1.53 - 1.00 = 0.53\ \text{MJ}$.

**Answer:** (a) $\eta = 65.3$ percent. (b) $Q_H = 1.53\ \text{MJ}$ and $Q_C = 0.53\ \text{MJ}$.

**Sanity check:** The efficiency lies between 0 and 1. Heat in equals work out plus heat rejected, which holds: $1.53 = 1.00 + 0.53$. A real steam plant with the same temperatures would reach well under 65 percent, because of friction, heat leaks, and irreversible steps.

## Lessons for engineers today

These engineers worked with limited theory and built many things that worked. Smeaton's lighthouse stood on a rock exposed to the sea, and his work on lime mortar shows the value of testing a material for its actual use. Carnot showed that a limit exists before anyone built a machine that approached it. Good design uses the best known model, then checks it against measurements.

## Practice

1. A heat engine runs between 800 K and 300 K. Find its Carnot efficiency as a percentage.
2. A steam engine is rated at 5 hp. Convert the rating to watts, using 745.7 W per horsepower.
3. A reversible engine has $T_H = 400\ \text{K}$ and $T_C = 300\ \text{K}$. It must deliver 1.0 kJ of work. Find the heat it takes from the hot reservoir in kilojoules.
4. Short answer: Watt's separate condenser kept the cylinder hot. Why did that save fuel compared with Newcomen's engine?
5. Short answer: The original Bessemer process could not remove phosphorus. What did that limit mean for the ores used with it?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\eta = 1 - 300 / 800 = 0.625$, which is 62.5 percent.
2. $5 \times 745.7 = 3728.5\ \text{W}$, which is about 3.73 kW.
3. $\eta = 1 - 300/400 = 0.25$, so $Q_H = 1.0 / 0.25 = 4.0\ \text{kJ}$.
4. Newcomen's cylinder cooled on every stroke and then had to be reheated. Watt's separate condenser cooled the steam in a different vessel, so the cylinder stayed hot. The engine then needed less fuel per stroke.
5. The process could only use ores with low phosphorus. High-phosphorus iron ore produced steel that was brittle, so it could not be used with the original process until the basic lining of Thomas and Gilchrist in 1879.

## Common mistakes

- Saying Watt invented the steam engine. Newcomen's atmospheric engine was built in 1712, and Watt improved its efficiency.
- Treating the Carnot efficiency as a design target. It is an upper limit that no real engine reaches.
- Saying the *Rocket* started the railway age. The Stockton and Darlington Railway opened in 1825, four years before the Rainhill Trials.
- Assuming Brunel saw the Clifton Suspension Bridge finished. It opened in 1864, five years after he died.
- Crediting Eiffel alone for the tower. Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier designed it in Eiffel's office.
