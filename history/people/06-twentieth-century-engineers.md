# Twentieth-century engineers

**Type:** lesson

Twentieth-century engineers are the people who built the theory, machines, and systems behind aircraft, rockets, computers, transistors, and communication codes. Engineers today should know them because these inventions are still in daily use. Several of them also had controversial records, and those are covered briefly
and factually.

**Prerequisites:** [War, nuclear, and space](../08-war-nuclear-and-space.md), [Semiconductors and computing](../09-semiconductors-and-computing.md), [Algebra](../../math/01-algebra.md)
**Time:** 3-4 h

## Core ideas

### Theodore von Karman

Von Karman was born in Budapest in 1881 and worked in Germany before moving to the California Institute
of Technology in 1930. He described the alternating vortex pattern shed behind a bluff body in 1911 and
1912, now called the Karman vortex street. He co-founded Aerojet in 1942 and helped start the rocket
group that became the Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Engineers use vortex shedding to check bridges,
chimneys, and cables for wind-driven vibration. See
[Towers and cables](../../applications/structures/06-towers-and-cables.md) and
[Aircraft and wings](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/05-aircraft-and-wings.md).

### Robert Goddard

Goddard was born in Worcester, Massachusetts, in 1882 and taught physics at Clark University. On
16 March 1926 near Auburn, Massachusetts, he launched the first liquid-fueled rocket, which flew for
a few seconds. He patented multistage and liquid-propellant rocket ideas and worked through the 1930s
with small teams and limited funding. Modern rockets use liquid propellants, and NASA's Goddard Space
Flight Center is named after him. See [Rockets](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/07-rockets.md).

### Sergei Korolev

Korolev worked as the chief designer of the Soviet space program. His identity was kept secret until
his death in 1966. His team's R-7 rocket launched Sputnik 1 on 4 October 1957, and a derivative of the
R-7 launched Yuri Gagarin on 12 April 1961. Modern launch vehicles still use the staged, liquid-fueled
design that his teams refined. See [Spacecraft and satellites](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/08-spacecraft-and-satellites.md).

### Wernher von Braun

Von Braun was born in 1912 in what is now Poland and led the V-2 rocket program at Peenemunde in
Germany. The first successful V-2 launch took place on 3 October 1942, and the rocket was used against
targets in Britain and Belgium in 1944 and 1945. Production at the Mittelwerk factory relied on forced
labor from concentration camp prisoners, and many died building the rockets. Von Braun held an SS rank,
and historians have documented that he knew about the labor conditions. After the war the United States
brought him to work on missiles and then on the Saturn V rocket for the Apollo program. His rocket
designs are historically important, and his wartime record is a serious part of that history. See
[Rockets](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/07-rockets.md).

### Alan Turing

Turing was born in London in 1912 and worked at Bletchley Park during the Second World War. In 1936 he
published a paper defining a theoretical machine that reads and writes symbols on a tape, now called the
Turing machine. The paper defined what it means for a method to be computable. After the war he designed
the Automatic Computing Engine at the National Physical Laboratory in London. He died in 1954. The
theory of computation, which asks what a machine can and cannot compute, starts from his model. See
[Digital logic and computers](../../applications/electronics/07-digital-logic-and-computers.md).

### John von Neumann

Von Neumann was born in Budapest in 1903 and worked in the United States at Princeton and the Institute
for Advanced Study. In 1945 he wrote the "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC," which describes a
computer that stores its program and data in the same memory. The design came out of the ENIAC team,
including J. Presper Eckert and John Mauchly, but the report bore von Neumann's name. Nearly every
computer built since then follows this stored-program architecture. See
[Digital logic and computers](../../applications/electronics/07-digital-logic-and-computers.md).

### Claude Shannon

Shannon was born in Gaylord, Michigan, in 1916. In 1937 his MIT master's thesis showed that Boolean
algebra can describe relay switching circuits, which made digital logic design possible. In 1948 he
published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," which defined information and channel capacity. His
formula sets the highest rate at which a noisy channel can carry error-free data. Modems, Wi-Fi, and
cellular standards use error-correcting codes that approach this limit. See
[Radio and antennas](../../applications/waves-and-communication/02-radio-and-antennas.md) and
[Digital logic and computers](../../applications/electronics/07-digital-logic-and-computers.md).

### Grace Hopper

Hopper was born in New York in 1906 and served in the U.S. Navy. She programmed the Harvard Mark I and
then worked at Eckert-Mauchly and Remington Rand on the UNIVAC. In 1952 she wrote the A-0 system, one of
the first compilers, which translated symbolic instructions into machine code. She helped develop
COBOL in the late 1950s. Compilers still translate source code into machine instructions. See
[Digital logic and computers](../../applications/electronics/07-digital-logic-and-computers.md).

### John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley

Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley worked at Bell Laboratories in Murray Hill, New Jersey. Bardeen was
born in 1908 in Wisconsin, Brattain in 1902 in China, and Shockley in 1910 in London. In December 1947
Bardeen and Brattain demonstrated the point-contact transistor, which amplified a signal. In 1951
Shockley announced the junction transistor, which became the basis of most later transistors. The three
shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. Late in life Shockley promoted racist views on intelligence,
which scientists widely condemned. See
[Semiconductors, diodes, and transistors](../../applications/electronics/03-semiconductors-diodes-and-transistors.md).

### Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce

Kilby worked at Texas Instruments in Dallas, Texas, and Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor in California.
Kilby demonstrated the first integrated circuit in September 1958, built on germanium. In 1959 Noyce
designed a planar silicon integrated circuit at Fairchild (working chips followed in 1960), a design that could be manufactured by
photolithography. Noyce later co-founded Intel in 1968. Every chip today combines many transistors on a
single piece of silicon. See
[Semiconductors, diodes, and transistors](../../applications/electronics/03-semiconductors-diodes-and-transistors.md)
and [Printed circuit boards](../../applications/electronics/04-printed-circuit-boards.md).

### Kelly Johnson

Johnson was born in 1910 in Michigan and led the Lockheed Skunk Works in California. The U-2 spy plane
first flew in 1955, and the SR-71 Blackbird first flew in 1964. He kept small teams, set strict schedules,
and emphasized simple designs and fast prototyping. Modern aircraft programs still use his approach to
high-altitude and high-speed design. See [Aircraft and wings](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/05-aircraft-and-wings.md)
and [Aircraft structures](../../applications/structures/04-aircraft-structures.md).

### Fazlur Rahman Khan

Khan was born in 1929 in Dhaka, in what is now Bangladesh. He worked at Skidmore, Owings and Merrill in
Chicago and developed the tube structural system, in which the outer walls of a tall building act as a
rigid frame. The John Hancock Center, completed in 1969, and the Willis Tower, completed in 1973, use
this approach. He died in 1982. Tall buildings still use tube and bundled-tube systems. See
[Buildings and skyscrapers](../../applications/structures/02-buildings-and-skyscrapers.md).

### Katherine Johnson

Johnson was born in 1918 in West Virginia and worked at the NACA and then NASA Langley Research Center
in Virginia. She calculated trajectories for early American spaceflight, including the 1962 orbital
flight of John Glenn in Friendship 7. She also worked on Apollo mission calculations. She died in 2020.
Spaceflight still depends on trajectory calculations of the kind she did by hand and with mechanical
calculators. See [Spacecraft and satellites](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/08-spacecraft-and-satellites.md).

### Margaret Hamilton

Hamilton was born in 1936 and led the flight software effort for the Apollo guidance computer at MIT's
Instrumentation Laboratory. Her team's software handled overloads with priority-based scheduling. On
the Apollo 11 landing in 1969, the computer raised alarms, and the software kept the most important
tasks running. She helped establish software engineering as a discipline. Designers of flight software still study
how Apollo's software handled overloads. See
[Digital logic and computers](../../applications/electronics/07-digital-logic-and-computers.md).

## Key equations

Shannon's channel capacity sets the highest rate at which information can cross a channel with
bandwidth $B$ in hertz (Hz) and signal-to-noise power ratio $S/N$, which is dimensionless. The capacity
$C$ is in bits per second (bit/s).

$$C = B \log_2\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right)$$

Engineers often quote the ratio in decibels. The conversion is

$$\frac{S}{N} = 10^{\,(S/N)_{\text{dB}}/10}$$

A 3 dB increase doubles the power ratio, which adds about one bit per second for each hertz of
bandwidth at high signal-to-noise ratio.

## Worked example

**Given:** A telephone channel has a bandwidth $B = 3000$ Hz and a signal-to-noise ratio of 30 dB.

**Find:** The channel capacity $C$.

**Assumptions:** The noise is white Gaussian noise spread evenly across the band. The channel does not
change over time.

**Solution:**

The linear power ratio is $S/N = 10^{30/10} = 1000$.

The capacity is $C = 3000 \times \log_2(1 + 1000) = 3000 \times \log_2(1001) = 3000 \times 9.9672 = 29\,902$ bit/s.

**Answer:** The channel can carry about 29.9 kbit/s without errors, in the limit of ideal coding.

**Sanity check:** The result is about 10 bit/s for each hertz, which matches $\log_2(1001) \approx 10$.
A real modem coding close to the limit can approach this rate, and the result is the upper bound.

## Lessons for engineers today

Shannon's formula tells a designer what is achievable before any hardware exists. A designer who knows
the bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio can calculate the rate ceiling and decide whether a link is worth
building. The Turing and von Neumann models separate the program from the machine that runs it, which is
why one processor can run many applications. Johnson, Khan, and Hamilton each show that engineers must
design for loads, overloads, and failures they expect to see in service. Kilby's and Noyce's work shows
how integrating many small parts on one substrate can cut cost and raise reliability.

## Practice

1. A channel has bandwidth 1 MHz and a signal-to-noise ratio of 10 dB. Find the capacity.
2. A telephone channel has bandwidth 3000 Hz and a signal-to-noise ratio of 20 dB. Find the capacity.
3. Why is Shannon's capacity called an upper limit rather than a design?
4. Why does adding bandwidth not always raise capacity by the same factor?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. The linear ratio is $10^{1} = 10$. The capacity is $C = 10^6 \times \log_2(11) = 10^6 \times 3.4594 = 3.46$ Mbit/s.
2. The linear ratio is $10^{2} = 100$. The capacity is $C = 3000 \times \log_2(101) = 3000 \times 6.6582 = 19\,975$ bit/s,
   or about 20.0 kbit/s.
3. Shannon proved that no code can carry data reliably above $C$, and that codes exist which approach it.
   The formula does not say how to build such a code. Engineers design the code and the modulation to
   get close.
4. Noise power grows with bandwidth when the noise density is fixed, so the signal-to-noise ratio falls
   as the band widens. Capacity grows only as the logarithm of $1 + S/N$, so the gain shrinks.

</details>

## Common mistakes

- Hopper's team found a moth in the Harvard Mark II in 1947, and the story is often told as the origin
  of the word "bug." The word meant a fault in a machine long before that date.
- Turing invented the computer alone. His 1936 paper defined computability, but the Colossus codebreaking
  machine was designed largely by Tommy Flowers at the Post Office Research Station.
- Von Neumann designed the stored-program computer alone. His 1945 EDVAC report summarized work from the
  ENIAC team, including Eckert and Mauchly.
- Shannon's formula gives a code. It gives a limit, and the code must be designed separately.
- The transistor came from a single lucky discovery. It came from a planned search at Bell Labs for a
  solid-state amplifier to replace vacuum tubes.
