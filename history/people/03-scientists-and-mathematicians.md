# Scientists and mathematicians

**Type:** lesson

**Scientists and mathematicians** are the thinkers, active from about 1650 to 1920, who wrote the equations that engineers still use. Engineers today should know them because those equations still size beams, pipes, circuits, and engines in real designs. This lesson covers fourteen people in rough order of their work, and each entry links to the lesson in this repo that uses it.

**Prerequisites:** [Scientific revolution](../04-scientific-revolution.md), [Single-variable calculus](../../math/03-single-variable-calculus.md), [Bending and buckling](../../physics/05-bending-and-buckling.md), [Heat transfer](../../physics/12-heat-transfer.md), [Hydrostatics and Bernoulli](../../physics/10-hydrostatics-and-bernoulli.md)
**Time:** 4-6 h

## Core ideas

Each person below gets a short entry. It says when and where the person worked, what they contributed, and where engineers use that work today.

### Robert Hooke, 1635 to 1703

Hooke worked in London, where he was curator of experiments at the Royal Society. He hid the spring law in an anagram in 1676, then published its meaning, "ut tensio, sic vis" (as the extension, so the force), in 1678. For a spring, force is proportional to extension, so $F = kx$. Engineers use it for springs, the stiffness of bars, and the elastic range of a material, which ends at the **proportional limit**. See [Mechanical properties](../../materials/03-mechanical-properties.md) and [Gears, fasteners, and springs](../../design/09-gears-fasteners-and-springs.md).

### Christiaan Huygens, 1629 to 1695

Huygens was born in The Hague in the Dutch Republic. He built a pendulum clock in 1656, the first clock in which a pendulum controlled the timekeeping. In 1690 he published the wave theory of light in *Traité de la lumière*. Engineers use his work in timekeeping and in wave optics, including lens and laser design. See [Clocks and watches](../../applications/everyday/07-clocks-and-watches.md) and [Waves, light, and heat](../../physics/07-waves-light-heat.md).

### Isaac Newton, 1642 to 1727

Newton worked in England, mostly at Cambridge and later in London. His *Principia*, published in 1687, states the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation. He also developed calculus in the 1660s and 1670s, though he published it later. Every force balance and every motion model in engineering rests on his laws. See [Particle dynamics](../../physics/02-particle-dynamics.md) and [Single-variable calculus](../../math/03-single-variable-calculus.md).

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, 1646 to 1716

Leibniz worked in Hanover and other German courts. He published differential calculus in 1684 with the notation $dx$ for a small change, and he introduced the integral sign $\int$ in print in 1686. That notation is the one students use today. Engineers use it in every textbook, spreadsheet, and solver that handles rates and areas. See [Single-variable calculus](../../math/03-single-variable-calculus.md).

### The Bernoullis: Jakob, Johann, and Daniel

The Bernoulli family were mathematicians from Basel in Switzerland. Jakob Bernoulli (1654 to 1705) published *Ars Conjectandi* in 1713, which contains the law of large numbers. Johann Bernoulli (1667 to 1748) taught Euler. Johann's son Daniel Bernoulli (1700 to 1782) published *Hydrodynamica* in 1738, which gives the link between pressure and speed along a streamline. Engineers use Bernoulli's principle to size pipes, check pumps, and build flow meters, and they use the probability work for reliability and risk. See [Hydrostatics and Bernoulli](../../physics/10-hydrostatics-and-bernoulli.md), [Fluid systems](../../applications/thermal-fluid/02-fluid-systems.md), and [Probability and statistics](../../math/07-probability-and-statistics.md).

### Leonhard Euler, 1707 to 1783

Euler was born in Basel and worked in St Petersburg and Berlin. He was one of the most productive mathematicians ever. In 1744 he published the elastic curve and the **critical load** at which a slender column buckles. He also wrote the equations of inviscid fluid flow in 1757, and he popularized the notation for $e$, $\pi$, and $f(x)$ that engineers still use. The Euler-Bernoulli beam theory carries his name and Daniel Bernoulli's. Engineers use his buckling formula to design columns, struts, and the legs of machines. See [Bending and buckling](../../physics/05-bending-and-buckling.md) and [Differential equations](../../math/06-differential-equations.md).

### Joseph-Louis Lagrange, 1736 to 1813

Lagrange was born in Turin and worked in Berlin and then Paris. His *Mécanique analytique*, published in 1788, rewrote mechanics using energy and general coordinates. The Lagrange equations derived from it give the motion of linked systems without drawing every force. Engineers use them for robot arms, multibody vehicles, and vibrating structures. See [Vibrations](../../physics/13-vibrations.md) and [Robot arms](../../applications/machines-robots/01-robot-arms.md).

### Joseph Fourier, 1768 to 1830

Fourier was a French mathematician who also held administrative posts, and he worked on the heat problem for many years. His *Théorie analytique de la chaleur* appeared in 1822. It states that heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient, and it introduced the series expansion that now bears his name. Engineers use Fourier's law to size insulation, heat exchangers, and electronics heat sinks, and they use Fourier analysis to study vibration and signals. See [Heat transfer](../../physics/12-heat-transfer.md) and [Mathematics reference](../../math/reference.md).

### Carl Friedrich Gauss, 1777 to 1855

Gauss worked at Göttingen in Germany. His *Theoria motus*, published in 1809, sets out least squares for fitting a model to noisy measurements. Legendre published the method first, in 1805, so the priority is disputed. Gauss also gave the normal distribution its central role in error analysis, and the elimination method for linear systems now called Gaussian elimination is named after him. Engineers use least squares to fit calibration curves, and they use elimination inside every structural and circuit solver. See [Linear algebra](../../math/05-linear-algebra.md) and [Probability and statistics](../../math/07-probability-and-statistics.md).

### Claude-Louis Navier, 1785 to 1836, and George Gabriel Stokes, 1819 to 1903

Navier was a French engineer who wrote the equations for the motion of viscous fluids in 1822. Stokes was born in Ireland and became a professor at Cambridge. He derived the same equations in 1845 and gave his drag law for a sphere in 1851. Together they form the **Navier-Stokes equations**, which describe air and water flow. Engineers use them in computational fluid dynamics for aircraft, pumps, and cooling. See [Fluid systems](../../applications/thermal-fluid/02-fluid-systems.md) and [Control volumes and pipe flow](../../physics/11-control-volumes-pipe-flow.md).

### James Clerk Maxwell, 1831 to 1879

Maxwell was born in Edinburgh and worked in Aberdeen, London, and Cambridge. In 1861 to 1865 he published the equations that unify electricity and magnetism. They predict that electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light. He was the first Cavendish Professor at Cambridge, and the Cavendish Laboratory opened under him in 1874. Engineers use Maxwell's equations to design antennas, motors, transformers, and shielding. See [Electricity and magnetism](../../physics/06-electricity-and-magnetism.md) and [Radio and antennas](../../applications/waves-and-communication/02-radio-and-antennas.md).

### Ludwig Boltzmann, 1844 to 1906

Boltzmann was born in Vienna and worked in Austria and Germany. He linked entropy to the number of microscopic arrangements of a gas, with $S = k_B \ln W$. The constant $k_B$ is now fixed at exactly $1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$. Engineers use the kinetic theory and the entropy view of the second law to explain why heat engines cannot convert all heat into work. See [Thermodynamics: second law and cycles](../../physics/09-thermo-second-law-cycles.md) and [Thermodynamics: first law](../../physics/08-thermo-first-law.md).

### Emmy Noether, 1882 to 1935

Noether was born in Erlangen and worked at Göttingen in Germany. In 1918 she proved **Noether's theorem**: every continuous symmetry of a physical system gives a conservation law. Time symmetry gives conservation of energy, and space symmetry gives conservation of momentum. Engineers rely on these conservation laws to check simulations and to build energy and momentum balances into every model. See [Particle dynamics](../../physics/02-particle-dynamics.md).

## Key equations

Hooke's law for a spring:

$$F = kx$$

where $F$ is force in newtons (N), $k$ is stiffness in newtons per metre (N/m), and $x$ is extension in metres (m).

Fourier's law of heat conduction:

$$\dot q = -k \frac{dT}{dx}$$

where $\dot q$ is heat flux in watts per square metre ($\text{W/m}^2$), $k$ is thermal conductivity in watts per metre kelvin (W/(m K)), and $dT/dx$ is the temperature gradient in kelvin per metre (K/m).

Euler's critical buckling load for a slender column:

$$P_{\text{cr}} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{(KL)^2}$$

where $P_{\text{cr}}$ is the critical load in newtons (N), $E$ is the elastic modulus in pascals (Pa), $I$ is the second moment of area in metres to the fourth power ($\text{m}^4$), $L$ is the column length in metres (m), and $K$ is the effective length factor (dimensionless).

Navier-Stokes equations for incompressible flow:

$$\rho\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}\right) = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf{v} + \rho\mathbf{g}$$

where $\rho$ is density in $\text{kg/m}^3$, $\mathbf{v}$ is velocity in m/s, $p$ is pressure in Pa, $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity in Pa s, and $\mathbf{g}$ is gravitational acceleration in $\text{m/s}^2$.

## Worked example

**Given:** A solid round steel column, 3.0 m long, with a diameter of 50 mm. Both ends are pinned, so $K = 1$. Take $E = 200\ \text{GPa}$, a typical value for steel.

**Find:** (a) the critical buckling load, (b) the critical stress, and (c) the slenderness ratio $KL/r$.

**Solution:**

1. Second moment of area: $I = \pi d^4 / 64 = \pi (0.050)^4 / 64 = 3.07 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$.
2. Critical load: $P_{\text{cr}} = \pi^2 E I / (KL)^2 = \pi^2 (200\times10^9)(3.07\times10^{-7}) / (1.0 \times 3.0)^2 = 6.73 \times 10^4\ \text{N}$, which is 67.3 kN.
3. Area: $A = \pi d^2 / 4 = 1.963 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$.
4. Critical stress: $\sigma_{\text{cr}} = P_{\text{cr}} / A = 67.3 \times 10^3 / 1.963 \times 10^{-3} = 3.43 \times 10^7\ \text{Pa}$, which is 34.3 MPa.
5. Radius of gyration: $r = \sqrt{I/A} = 0.0125\ \text{m}$, so $KL/r = 3.0 / 0.0125 = 240$.

**Answer:** (a) $P_{\text{cr}} = 67.3\ \text{kN}$. (b) $\sigma_{\text{cr}} = 34.3\ \text{MPa}$. (c) $KL/r = 240$.

**Sanity check:** The critical stress of 34.3 MPa is well below the yield strength of mild steel, which is about 250 MPa (typical). That fits a slender column that fails by buckling, not by yielding. If the column were fixed at one end and free at the other, $K = 2$ and the load drops by a factor of four to 16.8 kN.

## Lessons for engineers today

Euler's buckling result is still the first check on any slender member, from a bicycle frame to a crane leg. Fourier's conduction law sets the first estimate for every insulation and heat sink. The Navier-Stokes equations are the reason computational fluid dynamics exists, and Maxwell's equations are the reason every wireless device works. Each of these people wrote a model, then tested it against measurements. Engineers still work that way.

## Practice

1. A steel column is 2.0 m long, pinned at both ends, with a diameter of 30 mm. Take $E = 200\ \text{GPa}$ and find $P_{\text{cr}}$ in kilonewtons.
2. A spring has stiffness $k = 400\ \text{N/m}$. It stretches by 0.050 m. Find the force in newtons.
3. A brick wall is 0.20 m thick, with thermal conductivity $k = 0.80\ \text{W/(m K)}$. The inside is 20 K warmer than the outside. For 1.0 $\text{m}^2$ of wall, find the heat flow in watts, using $q = kA\,\Delta T / L$.
4. Water flows at 0.50 m/s in a horizontal pipe, then speeds up to 3.0 m/s at a narrow section. The density is 1000 $\text{kg/m}^3$. Use Bernoulli's principle to find the pressure drop in pascals, ignoring friction.
5. Short answer: A short, thick column fails by yielding, not buckling. Why does the Euler formula not apply to it?

1. $P_{\text{cr}} = 19.6\ \text{kN}$
2. $F = 20\ \text{N}$
3. $q = 80\ \text{W}$
4. $\Delta p = 4375\ \text{Pa}$, which is about 4.4 kPa
5. The Euler formula assumes the column fails by elastic buckling. A short column reaches its yield stress before the Euler load, so the Euler formula gives a load that is too high. Engineers use the slenderness ratio to choose which check applies.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking Newton and Leibniz invented calculus alone. Both developed it independently, and they argued over priority for decades.
- Applying the Euler buckling formula to every column. It applies only to slender columns that buckle elastically. Short columns fail by yielding or crushing.
- Assuming Hooke's law holds at any load. It holds only up to the proportional limit of the material.
- Believing the Navier-Stokes equations are solved in general. Existence and smoothness of solutions for three-dimensional flow is still an open problem, so engineers solve them numerically.
- Confusing Bernoulli's principle with a claim that fast air always lifts a wing. Bernoulli's relation holds along a streamline with no added energy and no friction. Wing lift needs the full circulation and pressure analysis.
