# Electrical age pioneers

**Type:** lesson

The people in this lesson turned electricity and radio from lab curiosities into networks that run
cities and carry messages across oceans. Their motors, generators, grids, radios, and assembly lines
are still the building blocks engineers use today.

**Prerequisites:** [Industrial revolution](../05-industrial-revolution.md), [Electrical age](../06-electrical-age.md), [Electricity and magnetism](../../physics/06-electricity-and-magnetism.md), [Trigonometry](../../math/02-trigonometry.md)
**Time:** 3-4 h

## Core ideas

### Michael Faraday

Faraday lived in London from 1791 to 1867. He had little formal schooling, trained as a bookbinder,
and became a researcher at the Royal Institution. In 1821 he built a simple electric motor, and in
1831 he showed that a changing magnetic field induces a current in a nearby coil. Faraday also laid
out the laws of electrolysis in 1833 and 1834. Every generator, alternator, and transformer relies on
his law of induction. See [Generators and alternators](../../applications/electrical/04-generators-and-alternators.md),
[Transformers](../../applications/electrical/05-transformers.md), and
[Electricity and magnetism](../../physics/06-electricity-and-magnetism.md).

### Charles Babbage and Ada Lovelace

Babbage worked in London and Cambridge, England, in the 1820s to 1840s. He began the Difference
Engine, a machine to calculate and print mathematical tables, and from 1837 he designed the more
general Analytical Engine. The Analytical Engine would read punched cards and separate a store, which
holds numbers, from a mill, which performs operations. Neither machine was finished in his lifetime.
Ada Lovelace, daughter of the poet Lord Byron, translated a paper on the Analytical Engine by Luigi
Menabrea and added her own notes in 1843. Her Note G describes a step-by-step method for computing
Bernoulli numbers, often called the first published program. Babbage's store and mill are early versions of
the memory and processor split in every modern computer. See
[Digital logic and computers](../../applications/electronics/07-digital-logic-and-computers.md).

### Thomas Edison

Edison worked in New Jersey, at Newark and at his Menlo Park laboratory, and held over 1,000 U.S.
patents. In 1879 his team produced a carbon-filament lamp that burned for many hours. In 1882 his
Pearl Street Station in lower Manhattan began supplying direct current to customers. Earlier inventors
had built incandescent lamps long before Edison, so he improved a known idea more than he invented
it. His central power station model became the pattern for the utility grid, though direct current
limited how far power could travel. See [The power grid](../../applications/electrical/08-the-power-grid.md).

### Nikola Tesla

Tesla was born in Smiljan, in what is now Croatia. He worked in Budapest, Paris, and New York, and he
spent a short time at Edison's company in 1884. In 1887 and 1888 he filed patents for a polyphase
alternating current system and for an induction motor with no commutator. George Westinghouse
licensed these patents. Induction motors now drive most of the pumps, fans, and compressors in
industry. In 1943 a U.S. Supreme Court decision on radio patents relied in part on Tesla's earlier
patents. See [Induction motors](../../applications/electrical/03-induction-motors.md).

### George Westinghouse

Westinghouse built his company in Pittsburgh. He patented the air brake in 1869, which lets compressed
air apply the brakes on every car of a train at once. He backed alternating current over Edison's
direct current. His engineer William Stanley demonstrated a transformer-based AC system at Great
Barrington, Massachusetts, in 1886. In the 1890s Westinghouse's company built the generators and AC
equipment for the Niagara Falls hydroelectric project. Modern trains still use compressed-air brakes.
See [Trains](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/10-trains.md) and
[Transformers](../../applications/electrical/05-transformers.md).

### Charles Steinmetz

Steinmetz was born in Breslau, in what is now Wroclaw, Poland. He emigrated to the United States in
1889 and spent most of his career at General Electric in Schenectady, New York. In 1892 he published a
law for hysteresis loss in iron, a formula still called the Steinmetz equation. He also brought complex
numbers, written as phasors, into AC circuit analysis. Engineers use phasors daily, and they use
Steinmetz's loss equation to estimate heating in transformer and motor cores. See
[Transformers](../../applications/electrical/05-transformers.md) and
[Induction motors](../../applications/electrical/03-induction-motors.md).

### Guglielmo Marconi

Marconi was born in Bologna, Italy, in 1874, and built his early radio stations in Italy and England.
On 12 December 1901 he received a signal at Signal Hill, near St. John's, Newfoundland, sent from
Poldhu in Cornwall, England. The signal was the letter S in Morse code, and it crossed the Atlantic
Ocean. Marconi shared the 1909 Nobel Prize in Physics with Karl Ferdinand Braun. Radio, Wi-Fi, and
cellular networks all use radio waves that follow the same basic physics. See
[Radio and antennas](../../applications/waves-and-communication/02-radio-and-antennas.md).

### Orville and Wilbur Wright

Wilbur was born in 1867, and Orville in 1871. The brothers ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio. On
17 December 1903, at Kill Devil Hills near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, Orville flew the first powered,
controlled airplane for about 12 seconds over about 37 m (120 feet). They solved control with wing
warping and a movable rudder. Before the first flight they built a wind tunnel and measured lift and
drag on model wings. Modern aircraft still control roll, pitch, and yaw, the three axes the Wrights
worked to master. See [Aircraft and wings](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/05-aircraft-and-wings.md).

### Henry Ford

Ford was born on a farm near Dearborn, Michigan, in 1863. He founded the Ford Motor Company in 1903.
The Model T arrived in 1908, and in 1913 the company introduced a moving assembly line at its
Highland Park plant, which sharply cut the time to build one car. Ford did not invent the automobile,
which existed decades earlier, but he made cars cheap enough for a mass market. Ford also printed
antisemitic material in his Dearborn Independent newspaper during the 1920s. That part of his public
record is harmful, and it is part of the history. See [Cars](../../applications/vehicles-aerospace/02-cars.md) and
[Conveyors and automation](../../applications/machines-robots/06-conveyors-and-automation.md).

### Frank and Lillian Gilbreth

Frank Gilbreth, born in 1868, was a building contractor who filmed bricklayers and cut the number of
motions each worker made. Lillian Gilbreth, born in 1878, was a psychologist who earned her PhD at
Brown University in 1915. After Frank died in 1924, she continued the work alone. Together they named
basic motion elements "therbligs," an anagram of their name. Engineers use motion study and time study
in work design and ergonomics today. See
[Design for manufacturing and assembly](../../design/10-design-for-manufacturing-and-assembly.md).

## Key equations

Faraday's law gives the voltage induced in a coil. The EMF $\mathcal{E}$ is in volts (V), $N$ is the
number of turns (dimensionless), $\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux in webers (Wb), and $t$ is time in
seconds (s).

$$\mathcal{E} = -N \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

The flux through a coil of area $A$ (m$^2$) in a field $B$ (T) is $\Phi_B = B A\cos\theta$, where
$\theta$ is the angle in radians (rad) between the field and the coil's normal. A coil that spins at a
constant angular speed $\omega$ (rad/s) has $\theta = \omega t$. The voltage then becomes

$$\mathcal{E}(t) = N B A \omega \sin(\omega t)$$

The peak voltage is $\mathcal{E}_{\text{peak}} = N B A \omega$. The angular speed relates to the frequency
$f$ in hertz (Hz) by $\omega = 2\pi f$.

## Worked example

**Given:** A simple generator has $N = 200$ turns, a field $B = 0.5$ T, a coil area $A = 0.01$ m$^2$
(a 10 cm by 10 cm square), and a frequency $f = 50$ Hz.

**Find:** The peak EMF and the RMS EMF.

**Assumptions:** The field is uniform, and the coil spins at a constant speed. The coil has no
resistance, so the voltage is the open-circuit EMF.

**Solution:**

The angular speed is $\omega = 2\pi \times 50 = 314.16$ rad/s.

The peak EMF is $\mathcal{E}_{\text{peak}} = 200 \times 0.5 \times 0.01 \times 314.16 = 314.2$ V.

The RMS value is $314.2 / \sqrt{2} = 222.1$ V.

**Answer:** The peak EMF is about 314 V, and the RMS EMF is about 222 V.

**Sanity check:** The units work out. One tesla times one square metre is one weber, and a weber per
second is one volt, so $N B A \omega$ has units of volts. A coil at 50 Hz turns 50 times per second,
which is 3000 rpm. That speed is typical for a two-pole synchronous generator on a 50 Hz grid.

## Lessons for engineers today

Faraday's law explains why a generator must move, or the field must change, to make a voltage. The
same law explains why a transformer needs AC: a steady DC current produces no changing flux, so it
induces nothing in the secondary winding. Engineers still choose between DC and AC for each part of a
system. AC won the long-distance argument because transformers change voltage cheaply, and higher
voltage means lower current for the same power. The Gilbreths' work shows that measurement of a process
is the first step in improving it.

## Practice

1. A generator has $N = 500$ turns, $B = 0.3$ T, $A = 0.02$ m$^2$, and $f = 60$ Hz. Find the peak EMF.
2. The same generator from question 1 runs at half the frequency, 30 Hz. Find the peak EMF.
3. Explain why a transformer does not work on a steady DC supply.
4. Lovelace's Note G computes Bernoulli numbers on a machine that was never built. What does that
   tell you about the difference between designing an algorithm and building a computer?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. $\omega = 2\pi \times 60 = 376.99$ rad/s, so $\mathcal{E}_{\text{peak}} = 500 \times 0.3 \times 0.02 \times 376.99 = 1131$ V,
   or about 1.13 kV.
2. Peak EMF is proportional to $\omega$, so the peak falls by half to $1131 / 2 = 565.5$ V,
   or about 566 V.
3. A steady DC current gives a constant magnetic flux. Faraday's law needs a changing flux to induce
   a voltage, so the secondary winding sees no voltage.
4. Lovelace wrote a correct method for a machine that existed only on paper. An algorithm can be
   correct before any hardware can run it. Engineers design the method and the machine separately,
   then check that they match.

## Common mistakes

- Edison invented the electric light bulb. He improved an existing idea, and earlier inventors built
  incandescent lamps decades before him.
- Tesla invented radio. Marconi built the first long-distance radio system, and the 1943 court decision
  only drew on Tesla's earlier patents.
- Ford invented the automobile. Cars existed decades earlier. Ford's contribution was low-cost
  production with a moving assembly line.
- The Wright brothers were the first to fly at all. Others had flown before them, but the Wrights
  achieved sustained, controlled powered flight.
- Lovelace wrote the first program that ran on a computer. The Analytical Engine was never built, so
  her method was published but never executed on that machine.
