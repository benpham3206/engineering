# Canals and railways

**Type:** lesson

Canals and railways are the transport routes, built between 1825 and 1916, that turned slow, costly overland and sea routes into corridors that carried goods every day. Engineers still use the same basic tools to plan long routes, including grade control, cut-and-fill earthworks, water control, and freight costing. The projects in this lesson cut through land, lifted ships and trains over hills, and linked oceans and continents.

## Core ideas

### The Erie Canal

The problem. In the early 1800s, goods moved between the Atlantic coast and the interior of North America mostly by wagon over rough roads. Overland freight was slow and expensive, and the Appalachian mountains blocked the easiest route west from New York.

The engineering. The state of New York built a canal about 584 km long from the Hudson River at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo. The route followed the gentle ground between the Mohawk Valley and the lake, and it climbed the ridges with a flight of locks. Engineers used a simple survey level and hand tools. The original canal had 83 locks, and each lock raised or lowered boats between gated chambers by a fixed step. Aqueducts carried the canal over rivers, and dams fed it water from streams.

The scale. Construction ran from 1817 to 1825, and the canal opened on October 26, 1825. It was about 363 miles long, and at the time it was one of the largest public works in the United States.

What went wrong. Many laborers were immigrants, including many from Ireland. Work in the swamps and low ground exposed crews to malaria and other fevers, and the canal earned the nickname "Clinton's Ditch" after DeWitt Clinton, the governor who backed the project. Deaths among the crews are not well recorded.

How it changed the world. Freight that cost about \$100 a ton to move overland fell to about \$10 a ton by canal, according to figures that were widely cited at the time. New York City grew into the largest port in the country, and the canal opened the Great Lakes to settlers and trade. Canals in other states soon copied the model.

### The Suez Canal

The problem. Ships from Europe to India and East Asia sailed around the Cape of Good Hope in Africa. That route added thousands of kilometres and weeks of sailing. A direct water route across the Isthmus of Suez would shorten the trip.

The engineering. The canal is a sea-level channel about 193 km long with no locks, which means the Mediterranean and Red Sea levels are close enough that the water does not need to be lifted. Dredgers cut through sand, clay, and rock. Engineers built in a desert, using freshwater channels and workers' camps. Ships pass through in convoys that meet at passing bays.

The scale. Construction ran from 1859 to 1869, and the canal opened on November 17, 1869. The French diplomat Ferdinand de Lesseps promoted the canal and led the company that built it. The canal shortened the sea route from Europe to Asia by thousands of kilometres.

What went wrong. Egyptian laborers worked under a forced labor system that the company and the government arranged. Conditions were harsh, and disease and accidents killed many workers. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, and the figures are disputed. The canal was closed during the Six-Day War of 1967 and reopened in 1975. Egypt nationalized the canal in 1956, which triggered a crisis that involved Britain, France, and Israel.

How it changed the world. Trade between Europe and Asia shifted toward steamships using the canal. In 2015 Egypt opened a second channel alongside the original route, which doubled capacity at certain points. In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given ran aground across the channel for about six days and blocked traffic until refloating.

### The Panama Canal

The problem. Ships sailing between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans had to go around Cape Horn at the tip of South America, a dangerous passage. Crossing the Isthmus of Panama promised a route far shorter than that voyage, but the land was mountainous, the climate was wet, and disease was common.

The engineering. The canal is about 82 km long, and it uses locks to lift ships about 26 m above sea level and lower them again on the other side. The lifts sit at three lock sites: Gatun on the Atlantic side, where three chambers in a row raise ships the full 26 m, and Pedro Miguel and Miraflores on the Pacific side, which lower ships in one and two steps. Engineers dammed the Chagres River to create Gatun Lake, which supplies the water for each lockage. The Culebra Cut required removing large volumes of rock and soil through the continental divide. The original locks measured 33.5 m wide and about 305 m long.

The scale. The French company attempted a sea-level canal from 1881 to 1889 and failed. Estimates of the deaths during that effort run to about 22,000, though the figure is uncertain. The United States took over the project and completed the canal in 1914, which is when it opened to traffic on August 15, 1914. The canal was widened with a third set of locks, which opened in 2016 for larger ships.

What went wrong. Yellow fever and malaria killed thousands of workers during both attempts. The French effort failed in large part because of disease and financial trouble. American engineers worked with physicians, including Walter Reed, whose commission showed that mosquitoes spread yellow fever. Sanitation and mosquito control then reduced the death toll sharply. The Culebra Cut suffered landslides for years after the canal opened.

How it changed the world. Ships no longer had to sail around South America to link the two oceans. Trade patterns and shipping routes shifted, and the canal remains one of the most important links in global shipping.

### The First Transcontinental Railroad

The problem. The United States needed a rail link between the Mississippi Valley and the Pacific coast. Wagon trains took months, and ships took longer. Building the line meant crossing the Great Plains, the Rocky Mountains, and the Sierra Nevada.

The engineering. Two companies built from opposite ends. The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and climbed the Sierra Nevada, which required tunnels, cuts, and trestles. The Union Pacific started in Omaha, Nebraska, and laid track west across the plains. Both lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah, where the last spike was driven on May 10, 1869. Crews used black powder for blasting and steam shovels for cuts.

The scale. The line covered about 3,000 km. Construction ran from 1863 to 1869. Crews in the Sierra Nevada used tunnels and large quantities of explosives, and the grade up the mountains was among the steepest on the route.

What went wrong. Most of the Central Pacific's workers were Chinese immigrants, who made up a large majority of the crew, and they faced hazardous blasting and avalanches in the mountains. Deaths among these workers were not fully recorded. The Union Pacific employed many Irish immigrants and veterans of the Civil War. The railroad's land grants and routes also displaced Indigenous nations, and the tracks cut through their lands without consent.

How it changed the world. Crossing the country fell from months to days. The railroad needed uniform timekeeping, and American railroads adopted standard time zones on November 18, 1883. The line also set a pattern of federal support for infrastructure that later projects followed.

### The Trans-Siberian Railway

The problem. Russia had a vast Pacific coast with a small population and poor land access. Moving troops, settlers, and goods from European Russia to the Far East by sea took months, and the sea route froze in winter.

The engineering. Construction began in 1891 under Tsar Alexander III. The main line runs from Moscow to Vladivostok, about 9,300 km long. Engineers laid track across the Ural Mountains, the Siberian plains, and the rivers that cross them, using bridges and ferries where needed. Between Lake Baikal and the Pacific, the line crosses rough mountain and river terrain, and some sections needed tunnels and long bridges. A shorter route through Manchuria, built by the Chinese Eastern Railway, was completed around 1903.

The scale. The main line was completed in 1916, which made it one of the longest railway lines in the world at that time. The construction effort relied on workers including convicts and soldiers, and conditions in the Siberian climate were harsh.

What went wrong. Disease, cold, and remote supply lines made work difficult, and the death toll is poorly recorded. The single track limited capacity, and during the Russo-Japanese War of 1904 to 1905 the line could not move troops and supplies fast enough to match Japanese forces.

How it changed the world. The railway opened Siberia to settlers and industry, and it tied the Russian Far East to the rest of the empire. It also made Vladivostok a major port for Russia.

## Key equations

The force that a train must overcome on a grade comes from the component of weight along the slope:

$$F_{\text{grade}} = m g \sin\theta \approx m g G$$

where $F_{\text{grade}}$ is the grade force in newtons (N), $m$ is the train mass in kilograms (kg), $g$ is the local gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s$^2$ at Earth's surface), $\theta$ is the angle of the slope, and $G$ is the grade, the rise over the run as a dimensionless ratio. For grades below about 5 percent, $\sin\theta \approx G$.

Assumptions: the track is straight, the grade is constant, the train moves at constant speed, and rolling resistance and air drag are ignored. The estimate gives only the part of the force due to gravity.

The energy needed to raise a load by height $h$ is:

$$E = m g h$$

where $E$ is the energy in joules (J), and $h$ is the height in metres (m). Assumptions: the energy goes entirely into potential energy, with no losses in the locomotive or the track.

For a lock, the water needed to raise a boat depends on the chamber area and the lift:

$$V = A_{\text{chamber}}\, h_{\text{lift}}$$

where $V$ is the volume of water in cubic metres (m$^3$), $A_{\text{chamber}}$ is the chamber's plan area in square metres (m$^2$), and $h_{\text{lift}}$ is the lift in metres (m). Assumptions: the chamber walls are vertical, the chamber area is constant over the lift, and leakage through the gates is neglected.

## Worked example

**Given:** A 1000 t freight train, so $m = 1.0 \times 10^6\ \text{kg}$, climbs a 1 km section with a constant grade of 1 percent. Assume the train travels at 20 m/s on the climb, and ignore rolling resistance and air drag for this estimate. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ (Earth surface).

**Find:** (a) the grade force the locomotives must overcome, (b) the energy needed to raise the train 10 m, and (c) the average power from the grade alone.

**Solution:**

1. Grade force: $F = m g G = 1.0 \times 10^6 \times 9.81 \times 0.01 = 9.81 \times 10^4\ \text{N}$, which is 98.1 kN.
2. Height gained: $h = G \times 1000\ \text{m} = 10\ \text{m}$.
3. Energy: $E = m g h = 1.0 \times 10^6 \times 9.81 \times 10 = 9.81 \times 10^7\ \text{J}$, which is 98.1 MJ, or about 27.3 kWh.
4. Time on the climb: $t = 1000 / 20 = 50\ \text{s}$.
5. Average power: $P = E / t = 9.81 \times 10^7 / 50 = 1.96 \times 10^6\ \text{W}$, which is about 2.0 MW.

**Answer:** (a) 98.1 kN. (b) 98.1 MJ, or about 27 kWh. (c) About 2.0 MW from the grade alone.

**Sanity check:** A 1 percent grade is gentle, but the train still needs about 100 kN just to hold its speed up the hill. A diesel locomotive of that era produced a few megawatts, so 2 MW from the grade alone is a large but plausible share of total power. The true power is higher once rolling resistance and air drag are included.

## Design trade-offs

Railway engineers keep grades low, because heavier grades need more locomotives or slower trains. Canal engineers choose between locks and long cuts. Locks lift boats cheaply in material but use water and slow traffic. A long cut avoids locks but needs more excavation. Both projects balance cost against capacity, and the Panama Canal's three-site design put lifts where water supply and rock conditions allowed them.

## How it fails

Canals fail when banks breach, when sediment fills the channel, or when a lock gate leaks. The Culebra Cut slid into the channel in the decades after the Panama Canal opened, and dredging remained a constant job. Railways fail when track washes out in floods, when grades exceed what the locomotives can pull, or when a single track jams traffic. Accidents during construction, including blasting and tunnel collapses, were a leading cause of worker deaths on many lines. Those hazards map onto the hierarchy of controls. Elimination removes the hazard, for example by routing a line around a tunnel. Substitution swaps black powder for safer explosives and replaces hand drilling with machine drills. Engineering controls add ventilation in tunnels, drainage, and bracing for cuts. Administrative controls set blasting schedules, crew limits, and clear warnings before detonation. Personal protective equipment, such as hard hats and eye protection, is the last layer, and it protects only the worker wearing it. Disease was controlled the same way, from removing mosquito breeding water at Panama, which is elimination, to screens and quarantine, which are administrative controls.

## History

The Erie Canal opened in 1825 and was the first major canal that linked the Great Lakes to the Atlantic. The Suez Canal opened in 1869, and the Panama Canal opened in 1914. The first transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869, and the Trans-Siberian Railway was completed in 1916. Each project relied on earlier advances in surveying, earth moving, and steam power, and each one took decades from planning to opening.

## Lessons for engineers today

Route choice sets the cost. A canal or railway that avoids steep grades and deep rock cuts costs less to run for a century. Disease, safety, and labor conditions decide whether a project finishes, and both the Panama and Suez projects show what happens when they are ignored. Early projects also show that a large public work can reshape a country's trade and settlement, so the political and social costs need the same planning as the civil works.

## Common mistakes

- The Panama Canal was built only by the French. The French company failed in the 1880s, and the United States completed the canal in 1914.
- The Suez Canal was the first canal to close. It was closed during the 1967 war and again during later conflicts, and the 2021 blockage by the Ever Given was not the first.
- The first transcontinental railroad was built by one company. Two companies built from opposite ends and met at Promontory Summit, Utah.
- Railways set standard time by law in 1883. The railroads adopted time zones in 1883, and federal law followed later.
- Locks lift ships using pumps. They fill and empty by gravity from a higher water level, and pumps are used only where a water supply needs extra help.
