# Wartime and science megaprojects

**Type:** lesson

Between 1940 and 2008, governments paid for projects too large and too risky for one firm or one laboratory. Radar, the Manhattan Project, Apollo, the International Space Station, and the Large Hadron Collider each set a new scale for engineering, and each produced costs and harms that engineers still study.

**Prerequisites:** [Electrical age](../06-electrical-age.md), [War, nuclear, and space](../08-war-nuclear-and-space.md), [Orbital mechanics and rockets](../../physics/15-orbital-mechanics-and-rockets.md)
**Time:** 3 to 4 hours

## Core ideas

### Radar and the MIT Radiation Laboratory

The problem. In 1940, Britain faced German bombers and submarines that were hard to find at night and in bad weather. Early radar used wavelengths of several metres. Long waves give wide beams and coarse angles, so the operator cannot separate one aircraft from another or find a small target. Engineers wanted centimetre waves, which give narrow beams and small antennas.

The engineering. At the University of Birmingham, John Randall and Harry Boot built a cavity magnetron in 1940. It produced pulses of centimetre-wavelength radio power at about 3 GHz, a wavelength of about 10 cm. The Tizard Mission carried this device to the United States later in 1940. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology opened the Radiation Laboratory that October to turn the device into working radar sets for aircraft, ships, and ground stations.

The scale. The laboratory grew to several thousand staff. It designed many radar systems for air defence, bombing, navigation, and ship and submarine detection, and its wartime work filled a long series of technical books.

What went wrong. Radar could be jammed and fooled. In July 1943, the Royal Air Force dropped strips of metal foil, called window, over Hamburg. The strips returned false echoes that filled German radar screens. Ground clutter from hills and buildings also masked real targets, so designers had to filter the displays.

How it changed the world. The magnetron and radar led to air traffic control, weather radar, and the microwave oven. Radar also made night and all-weather flying safer for civil aircraft after the war. Radar helped Britain defend its skies in 1940 and 1941. The same technology guided bombers over German and Japanese cities, and the bombing campaigns killed hundreds of thousands of civilians.

### The Manhattan Project

The problem. In 1939, physicists reported that uranium fission could release energy in a chain reaction, and a letter from Albert Einstein to President Franklin Roosevelt that year warned that Germany might build a bomb. The United States had to separate enough fissile material and build a weapon before the war ended. No single site had the equipment, so the work spread over several places.

The engineering. Three main sites carried the work. At Oak Ridge, Tennessee, engineers built gaseous diffusion plants and electromagnetic separators to enrich uranium. At Hanford, Washington, the Army built plutonium production reactors, and the first of them, the B Reactor, ran in September 1944. At Los Alamos, New Mexico, a team led by J. Robert Oppenheimer designed two weapons. One was a gun-type uranium bomb, and the other was an implosion-type plutonium bomb. The implosion design needed precise explosive lenses and timing.

The scale. About 130,000 people worked on the project at its peak. Its cost was about 2 billion dollars in 1945 dollars.

What went wrong. Two scientists died in criticality accidents at Los Alamos. Harry Daghlian died in 1945 after a reactor-core assembly went critical, and Louis Slotin died in 1946 after a similar accident. Radioactive releases and waste from the Hanford reactors contaminated land and water for decades, and cleanup of the underground waste tanks continues.

The human cost. The atomic bombs killed tens of thousands of people in Hiroshima and Nagasaki within months, and many more died later from injuries and radiation. Estimates vary. The conventional firebombing of Tokyo in March 1945 killed roughly 100,000 people, by most estimates.

How it changed the world. The project created the nuclear weapons that shaped postwar deterrence. It also produced reactor engineering and the first large-scale nuclear power plants, which came later. The Manhattan Project showed that a government could fund and manage thousands of specialists on one deadline.

### The Apollo program

The problem. In a speech to Congress on 25 May 1961, President John F. Kennedy set a goal of landing people on the Moon and returning them safely before the end of the decade. The United States needed a rocket that could lift a large payload to lunar distances, a spacecraft that could carry crew there and back, and a lander that could touch down and lift off again.

The engineering. The Saturn V rocket had three stages, stood about 110 m tall, and produced about 35 MN of thrust at liftoff. Engineers chose lunar orbit rendezvous. In this plan, a command and service module stayed in lunar orbit while a separate lunar module descended to the surface and returned to rendezvous. This plan cut the mass that had to reach the Moon and return to Earth. The Apollo Guidance Computer handled navigation and control with very little memory.

The scale. NASA and its contractors employed several hundred thousand people at peak. The program cost roughly 25 billion dollars in the dollars of the time.

What went wrong. On 27 January 1967, a fire in the Apollo 1 command module during a launch rehearsal killed Virgil Grissom, Edward White, and Roger Chaffee. The cabin used a pure oxygen atmosphere at pressure, and flammable materials and wiring faults fed the fire. On 13 April 1970, an oxygen tank in the service module of Apollo 13 ruptured. The crew moved into the lunar module and used it as a lifeboat, and all three astronauts returned safely.

How it changed the world. Apollo 11 landed on 20 July 1969, and Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the surface. The program advanced digital electronics, miniaturized computing, and materials engineering. Its lessons on systems engineering and test discipline still shape space projects.

### The International Space Station

The problem. After the Cold War, several space agencies wanted a permanently crewed laboratory in low Earth orbit. No single nation could fund it alone, so the partners agreed to build it together.

The engineering. Assembly started in orbit with the Russian-built Zarya module, launched on 20 November 1998. The American Unity module joined it in December 1998. Other modules followed over the next decade and a half. The station orbits at about 400 km altitude, takes about 92 minutes per orbit, and travels at about 7.7 km/s. Its mass is about 420 tonnes, and its solar arrays provide the electrical power. Partners include NASA, Roscosmos, the European Space Agency, the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, and the Canadian Space Agency.

The scale. Continuous human presence began on 2 November 2000. The total cost exceeds 100 billion dollars.

What went wrong. The Space Shuttle Columbia broke apart during reentry on 1 February 2003, and all seven crew members died. The shuttle fleet was grounded for more than two years, and assembly slowed during that time. Micrometeoroids and orbital debris remain a constant risk, and the station carries shielding and maneuvering capability for that reason.

How it changed the world. The station has hosted continuous crews for more than two decades and supports research in biology, materials, and physics in microgravity. Its partnership model became a template for later international projects. NASA plans to end its operation around 2030.

### The Large Hadron Collider

The problem. Particle physicists needed higher collision energies to test the Standard Model and to search for the Higgs boson. Higher energies require stronger bending magnets or a larger ring, and the ring had to fit under the Franco-Swiss border near Geneva.

The engineering. The Large Hadron Collider at CERN is a ring of superconducting magnets about 27 km in circumference, buried about 100 m underground. The dipole magnets bend the beams and run at about 1.9 K, colder than outer space, so the niobium-titanium coils carry current with no resistance. The design field is about 8.3 T at 7 TeV per beam. Since 2015, the beams have run at 6.5 TeV. Over a thousand dipole magnets steer the two counter-rotating proton beams.

The scale. The ring has about 27 km of circumference, more than a thousand dipole magnets, and a cryogenic system that holds the magnets near 1.9 K. Each beam stores hundreds of megajoules of energy, enough to melt a substantial amount of metal if it were released in an uncontrolled way.

What went wrong. On 19 September 2008, a faulty electrical joint between two magnets failed during the first high-energy operation. The resulting arc damaged magnets and released helium into the tunnel. Repairs and new protection systems delayed the restart until November 2009, about 14 months later. The protection system now detects quench events and dumps the stored magnetic energy safely.

How it changed the world. The experiments at the Large Hadron Collider announced the discovery of a Higgs-like boson on 4 July 2012. The collider also drove advances in superconducting magnet design, cryogenic engineering, and large-scale data processing and distributed computing.

## Key equations

Radar range equation. The maximum detection range of a radar is

$$R_{\max} = \left[\frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 P_{\min}}\right]^{1/4}$$

where $P_t$ is the transmitted power in watts, $G$ is the antenna gain (dimensionless), $\lambda$ is the wavelength in metres, $\sigma$ is the target radar cross section in square metres, and $P_{\min}$ is the minimum detectable received power in watts. The equation assumes one target, a single antenna for transmit and receive, free-space propagation with no atmospheric loss, and a noise-limited receiver. The fourth-root dependence means doubling the range needs sixteen times the transmitted power, all else equal.

Mass-energy relation. The energy released by a mass change is

$$E = \Delta m \, c^2$$

where $E$ is the energy in joules, $\Delta m$ is the mass lost in kilograms, and $c = 2.998 \times 10^8$ m/s is the speed of light in vacuum. The equation assumes all of the mass loss converts to energy. Real fission converts only a small fraction of the fissioned mass, about one tenth of a percent.

Tsiolkovsky rocket equation. The velocity change from a rocket burn is

$$\Delta v = v_e \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}$$

where $\Delta v$ is the velocity change in metres per second, $v_e$ is the exhaust speed in metres per second, $m_0$ is the initial mass in kilograms, and $m_f$ is the final mass in kilograms. The equation assumes a constant exhaust speed, an impulsive burn, and no gravity or drag losses.

Magnetic rigidity for a beam. The bending radius $r$ of a charged particle in a magnetic field is set by

$$B r = \frac{p}{q}$$

where $B$ is the field in tesla, $r$ is the radius in metres, $p$ is the momentum in kg m/s, and $q = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C is the proton charge. For a relativistic proton, $p c \approx E$, so $B \approx E / (c\, q\, r)$. The equation assumes the particle moves perpendicular to a uniform field.

Orbital speed. For a circular orbit,

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}$$

where $v$ is the orbital speed in metres per second, $\mu = 3.986 \times 10^{14}$ m$^3$/s$^2$ is the Earth gravitational parameter, and $r$ is the orbit radius from Earth's centre in metres. The equation assumes a point-mass Earth, no atmospheric drag, and no other perturbing bodies.

## Worked example

**Given:** A spacecraft parks in a circular Earth orbit at 185 km altitude. The Earth radius is 6371 km and its gravitational parameter is $\mu_E = 3.986 \times 10^{14}$ m$^3$/s$^2$. The Moon's mean distance from Earth is 384 400 km. The Moon's gravitational parameter is $\mu_M = 4.903 \times 10^{12}$ m$^3$/s$^2$, its radius is 1737.4 km, and the spacecraft enters a circular lunar orbit at 100 km altitude. Assume the spacecraft reaches the Moon with an excess speed of 0.8 km/s relative to the Moon.

**Find:** The ideal delta-v for four burns: trans-lunar injection (TLI), lunar orbit insertion (LOI), descent to the surface, and ascent back to a 100 km lunar orbit.

**Assumptions:**
1. All burns are impulsive, so the velocity changes instantly.
2. TLI is a Hohmann transfer from the parking orbit to an apogee at the Moon's mean distance. The Moon's own motion is ignored.
3. LOI is a capture burn from a hyperbolic approach with excess speed 0.8 km/s to a circular orbit at 100 km.
4. Descent uses a Hohmann-style ellipse with apolune at 100 km and perilune at the surface, then one burn at the surface to cancel horizontal speed. Ascent reverses that path.
5. The Moon does not rotate in this calculation, and the surface is treated as a sphere.
6. Gravity losses, steering losses, and mid-course corrections are ignored.

**Solution:**

1. Circular speed in the parking orbit: $r_1 = 6371 + 185 = 6556$ km, so $v_c = \sqrt{\mu_E / r_1} = 7.80$ km/s.
2. TLI: the transfer-ellipse perigee speed is $\sqrt{\mu_E\,(2/r_1 - 2/(r_1 + r_2))}$ with $r_2 = 384\,400$ km. Subtracting $v_c$ gives $\Delta v_{TLI} = 3.137$ km/s.
3. LOI: the Moon's periapsis radius is $r_p = 1737.4 + 100 = 1837.4$ km. The circular speed there is $\sqrt{\mu_M / r_p} = 1.634$ km/s. The hyperbolic periapsis speed is $\sqrt{v_\infty^2 + 2\mu_M / r_p} = 2.445$ km/s. So $\Delta v_{LOI} = 2.445 - 1.634 = 0.811$ km/s.
4. Descent: the first burn drops from the 100 km circle onto the descent ellipse, $\Delta v = 0.023$ km/s. The surface periapsis speed on that ellipse is 1.703 km/s, and the landing burn cancels it. Total descent is $0.023 + 1.703 = 1.726$ km/s.
5. Ascent: the surface burn of 1.703 km/s raises the vehicle onto the ellipse, and a circularizing burn of 0.023 km/s finishes the orbit. Total ascent is 1.726 km/s.
6. Sum: $3.137 + 0.811 + 1.726 + 1.726 = 7.400$ km/s.

**Answer:** The ideal total is about 7.4 km/s. TLI is 3.14 km/s, LOI is 0.81 km/s, descent is 1.73 km/s, and ascent is 1.73 km/s. The trans-Earth injection burn that sends the crew home is not included.

**Sanity check:** Escape from the parking orbit needs 3.23 km/s. TLI at 3.14 km/s is slightly less, which fits a transfer that stops at the Moon's distance, inside Earth's escape boundary. The surface circular speed of the Moon is 1.680 km/s. The ideal ascent of 1.703 km/s at the surface lies just above it, as expected for a burn that climbs to 100 km. Real budgets run higher because gravity losses and steering add delta-v, so the ideal total is a lower bound.

## Design trade-offs

Lunar orbit rendezvous saved mass by letting the lander carry only the descent and ascent stages to the surface. The trade-off added a rendezvous in lunar orbit, which carried risk. The Saturn V carried about 3000 tonnes at liftoff, and the mass budget set the rocket size.

Radar designers trade wavelength against antenna size. Shorter waves allow smaller antennas and narrower beams, but they need better magnetrons and more careful atmospheric analysis. Power and weight also compete, because aircraft have limited space.

The Large Hadron Collider trades magnetic field against cost. A stronger field gives higher energy in the same ring, but the superconducting magnets must stay near 1.9 K. Ring size is fixed by the tunnel, so the field is the main lever.

The International Space Station trades power against mass and crew time. Solar arrays give power but add mass and drag, and debris shielding adds mass that every launch must lift.

## Lessons for engineers today

Large projects need one clear owner for each interface. The Apollo program and the Manhattan Project both used a single program office to hold the schedule and the budget together.

Safety systems earn their cost in failures. The Apollo 1 fire, the Apollo 13 tank rupture, the Columbia accident, and the 2008 magnet joint failure each led to design changes that now protect every later mission or machine.

Every high-energy system stores more energy than its operators expect. The Los Alamos criticality accidents and the stored energy in the collider's beams both show that margins must be built into the hardware and into the procedures.

## Practice

2. Compute the energy released if 1 g of mass converts fully to energy, and express it in kilotons of TNT. Use 1 kt of TNT $= 4.184 \times 10^{12}$ J.
3. A rocket stage has an exhaust speed of 3000 m/s and a mass ratio $m_0/m_f = 4$. Find the velocity change using the Tsiolkovsky equation, assuming an impulsive burn and no losses.
4. The Large Hadron Collider runs 7 TeV per beam, and its arcs bend the beam on a radius of about 2.8 km. Use $B \approx E / (c\,q\,r)$ and find the dipole field.
5. Short answer. Why did the Apollo program choose lunar orbit rendezvous over landing the whole spacecraft on the Moon?

## Common mistakes

Treating the Manhattan Project as one laboratory. It used Oak Ridge, Hanford, Los Alamos, and several other sites, each with its own role.

Saying radar was invented in 1940. Several countries developed radar in the 1930s. The 1940 magnetron made centimetre-wavelength radar practical.

Calling the Apollo 1 disaster a launch failure. The fire happened on the pad during a rehearsal, before launch.

Claiming the Large Hadron Collider could destroy Earth by creating black holes. Cosmic rays strike the atmosphere with higher energies than the collider reaches, and the atmosphere shows no harm from them.

Thinking the International Space Station has no gravity. At 400 km, gravity is about 90 percent of its surface value. The crew lives in continuous free fall, which is why it feels weightless.
