# Bridges, dams, and towers

**Type:** lesson

Bridges, dams, and towers are the large structures that engineers built from the 1880s to the 2010s to set records for span, height, and power. Engineers today should know them because each one forced advances in steel and concrete, foundations, wind and seismic design, and construction safety. This lesson covers eight projects and the engineering each one needed.

## Core ideas

### The Brooklyn Bridge

The problem. New York City and the city of Brooklyn needed a permanent crossing of the East River. Ferries could not keep up with the traffic, and no bridge of that span had been built.

The engineering. John A. Roebling designed a suspension bridge with a main span of about 486 m, carried by steel wire cables anchored in masonry. Its towers rise above the water, and the deck hangs from the cables. The foundations of the towers were built with pneumatic caissons, large watertight chambers pushed into the riverbed with compressed air.

The scale. The main span of 486 m was the longest in the world when the bridge opened on May 24, 1883. Construction ran from 1869 to 1883.

What went wrong. John Roebling died in 1869, after a ferry accident crushed his foot and an infection set in. His son Washington Roebling took over, but he was struck by caisson disease, which is decompression sickness, and he was bedridden for years. Emily Warren Roebling carried messages between him and the crews and supervised much of the work. Commonly cited figures put the construction deaths at about 20, including workers who died from caisson disease.

How it changed the world. The bridge showed that steel wire could carry very long spans, and it influenced later suspension bridges. It also made Brooklyn part of New York City's commuting network.

### The Forth Bridge

The problem. Trains between Edinburgh and northern Scotland had to cross the Firth of Forth by ferry. The 1879 collapse of the Tay Bridge, which killed between 59 and 75 people (estimates vary), had shown the risks of a poorly designed crossing.

The engineering. John Fowler and Benjamin Baker designed a cantilever bridge. Each main span is built from two arms that reach out from a central pier, and the arms meet in the middle with a connecting girder. Steel tubes carry the compression, and the design resists wind through its large size and mass.

The scale. The main spans are about 521 m long. The bridge opened on March 4, 1890. The design needed large quantities of steel, and the tubes were riveted in place on site.

What went wrong. The official record listed 57 construction deaths; later research raised the count to 73. The bridge also needs constant maintenance against corrosion, and its painting work is never fully finished, which is a simplification of the familiar story.

How it changed the world. The bridge showed that cantilever steel could span very long distances. It also set a standard for steel design that many later bridges copied.

### The Eiffel Tower

The problem. The 1889 Exposition Universelle in Paris needed a landmark that would show the power of iron construction. The tower had to stand against wind and carry its own weight over a small base.

The engineering. Engineers in the firm of Gustave Eiffel, led by Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier, designed the tower from wrought iron lattice. The design uses thousands of riveted parts, and the four legs are braced by lattice beams. The legs curve, which makes the structure stiffer against the wind. Elevators brought visitors up the tower.

The scale. The tower stood about 300 m tall at its top when it opened in 1889, and the height is about 330 m today with antennas. It contains about 18,000 parts.

What went wrong. Many artists and writers protested that the tower was ugly, and the city permit ran for 20 years, after which the tower was to be dismantled. Radio antennas later saved it from demolition. Few worker deaths are recorded for the project.

How it changed the world. The tower showed what iron lattice could do at great height. Radio and later television antennas made the tower a long-lived communication site.

### The Golden Gate Bridge

The problem. San Francisco was cut off from the north by the strait of the Golden Gate, and ferries were slow and unreliable. The strait has strong tides and winds, and the water is deep.

The engineering. Joseph Strauss led the project, and engineers including Charles Ellis and Leon Moisseiff worked on the analysis. The bridge is a suspension bridge with a main span of about 1,280 m, and its towers rise about 227 m. The deck is stiffened by a deep truss that resists the wind and the traffic. Engineers gave the design careful attention to the wind after the 1940 failure of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge.

The scale. The bridge opened on May 27, 1937, and at the time its main span was the longest in the world.

What went wrong. Eleven workers died during construction. A scaffold under the deck fell in 1937, killing ten men. Before that accident, a safety net under the deck had caught 19 men in falls, which is a commonly cited figure.

How it changed the world. The bridge linked a major city with the north coast and became one of the most recognized structures in the United States. Its design influenced later suspension bridges with deep stiffening trusses.

### The Hoover Dam

The problem. The Colorado River flooded farms in the Imperial Valley, and the western states needed water storage and electric power for growing cities. The river runs through a deep canyon in Black Canyon, which offered a site for a large dam.

The engineering. The dam is a concrete arch-gravity structure about 221 m high. Engineers poured concrete in blocks and cooled the concrete with pipes carrying cold water, so that heat from curing did not crack the mass. The dam's generators have a combined capacity of about 2,080 MW. The reservoir, Lake Mead, stores the river's flow.

The scale. The dam used about 2.5 million cubic metres of concrete. President Franklin D. Roosevelt dedicated it on September 30, 1935, and it was completed in 1936.

What went wrong. Construction deaths are commonly cited as 112 in total, though the count depends on how deaths are classified. Heat, dust, and rock falls were the main hazards. The river's water and power were allocated under the Colorado River Compact of 1922, which remains disputed.

How it changed the world. The dam provided flood control, irrigation, and electricity for the American Southwest, and it supported the growth of cities including Las Vegas and Los Angeles.

### The Three Gorges Dam

The problem. The Yangtze River floods regularly, and its flow must be managed for navigation and power. Its gorges offered a site for a very large dam in central China.

The engineering. The gravity dam is about 185 m high, and it has a crest about 2.3 km long. Its powerhouse and spillways pass floodwater and drive generators, which together have a capacity of about 22,500 MW. A ship lift and locks allow navigation past the dam.

The scale. Construction began in 1994, the main structure was finished in 2006, and the last generators came online later. It is one of the largest power stations in the world.

What went wrong. Filling the reservoir required relocating more than one million people, and the resettlement caused hardship for many families. The dam changed sediment flow downstream and affected fish and river ecology. Its contribution to flood control is debated, and officials and critics disagree about the results.

How it changed the world. The dam supplies a large share of China's hydroelectric power and gives the river a regulated flow for navigation.

### The Akashi Kaikyo Bridge

The problem. The strait between Honshu and Awaji Island is wide, deep, and exposed to strong currents and earthquakes. Ferries had long carried traffic across it, and a bridge needed a span far longer than any built before.

The engineering. The suspension bridge has a main span of about 1,991 m, the longest in the world at its opening. The towers rise about 300 m. Engineers designed the deck to resist strong winds and the seismic forces of the region, and they built the deep caissons for the foundations using large steel and concrete structures.

The scale. The bridge opened on April 5, 1998. Its main span remains among the longest for any bridge.

What went wrong. The Kobe earthquake in January 1995 struck while the towers were under construction, and it moved them apart by about a metre. The design was revised to add that length to the span. Construction work in fast currents also carried serious hazards.

How it changed the world. The bridge linked two islands and two regions of Japan, and it set a benchmark for the engineering of very long spans in earthquake zones.

### The Burj Khalifa

The problem. Dubai wanted a tall tower that would become a symbol of the city and a center for business, and the tower's height created problems of wind, structure, and pumping concrete to great heights.

The engineering. The design uses a Y-shaped plan with three wings around a central core, which reduces wind forces by changing the way the wind wraps the tower. Engineers used high-strength concrete and steel in the core and the outer frame. The foundation rests on a large concrete raft supported by deep piles.

The scale. The tower is about 828 m tall and has 163 floors. It opened in January 2010 and is the tallest building in the world.

What went wrong. Labor rights groups and news reports described poor conditions and unpaid wages for some migrant workers on construction projects in Dubai, including this one. Construction at this height also raised the risks of falls and heavy lifts.

How it changed the world. The tower set new standards for high-rise concrete, wind engineering, and vertical transport. It also changed how cities used very tall buildings for commerce and tourism.

## Key equations

A suspension cable with a uniform load along the horizontal span takes a parabolic shape. The horizontal tension is:

$$H = \frac{w L^2}{8 h}$$

where $H$ is the horizontal tension in newtons (N), $w$ is the load per horizontal metre in newtons per metre (N/m), $L$ is the span in metres (m), and $h$ is the sag, the vertical drop from the tower tops to the lowest point of the cable, in metres (m).

The tension at the top of the tower combines the horizontal component with the vertical component from the load on half the span:

$$T = \sqrt{H^2 + \left(\frac{w L}{2}\right)^2}$$

where $T$ is the cable tension at the tower top in newtons (N).

Assumptions for both equations: the cable is perfectly flexible and carries no bending. The load is uniform per horizontal metre along the span. The towers are at the same height. The cable is in static equilibrium, so wind, traffic, and temperature effects are ignored. The sag is small compared with the span.

## Worked example

**Given:** The main span of the Golden Gate Bridge is $L = 1280\ \text{m}$. Assumptions for this estimate: sag $h = 143\ \text{m}$, and the total dead load carried by both main cables is $w = 60\ \text{kN/m}$ per horizontal metre. Each cable carries half the load, and each cable has the same tension. The towers are level, and the deck load is the only load considered.

**Find:** (a) the horizontal tension $H$, (b) the vertical component at the tower, (c) the tension at the tower top for both cables together, and (d) the tension per cable.

**Solution:**

1. Horizontal tension: $H = w L^2 / (8 h) = 60\,000 \times 1280^2 / (8 \times 143) = 8.59 \times 10^7\ \text{N}$, which is 85.9 MN.
2. Vertical component at the tower: $w L / 2 = 60\,000 \times 1280 / 2 = 3.84 \times 10^7\ \text{N}$, which is 38.4 MN.
3. Tension at the tower top for both cables: $T = \sqrt{(85.9)^2 + (38.4)^2}\ \text{MN} = 94.1\ \text{MN}$.
4. Per cable: $94.1 / 2 = 47.1\ \text{MN}$. That is about 4,800 tonne-force, since $47.1 \times 10^6 / 9.81 = 4.8 \times 10^6\ \text{kg}$ at Earth's surface gravity.

**Answer:** (a) $H = 85.9\ \text{MN}$. (b) $38.4\ \text{MN}$. (c) $T = 94.1\ \text{MN}$. (d) About $47\ \text{MN}$ per cable.

**Sanity check:** The sag ratio $h/L = 143/1280 = 0.11$ is a typical value for a suspension bridge, which means the cable is fairly flat and the horizontal tension is large. The tower-top tension is larger than the horizontal tension, as it must be, since it adds a vertical component. The result has units of force, and the magnitudes match the scale of a bridge that supports tens of thousands of tonnes of deck and traffic.

## Design trade-offs

A suspension bridge trades cable strength against sag. A deeper sag cuts horizontal tension, which lowers the anchorage load, but it makes the cable longer and the towers taller. A stiff deck resists wind and traffic loads, but it weighs more and adds cost. Dams trade height and storage against foundation cost and the displacement of people and ecosystems. Tall buildings trade floor area against wind loads, elevator capacity, and the cost of pumping concrete.

## How it fails

Suspension bridges can fail in wind when the deck starts to twist. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge failed in 1940 from aeroelastic torsional flutter, a self-excited motion in which the wind and the twisting deck feed energy into each other. The bridge had a shallow, light deck, and its torsional stiffness was too low to resist the motion. Dams fail by overtopping, by seepage under the foundation, or by cracking when concrete heats as it cures. Towers fail under earthquake loads, and foundations fail when soil settles.

Construction hazards have caused many of the deaths on these projects. Caisson disease, falls from height, collapse of scaffolding, heat, and rock falls are the main causes. A hierarchy of controls ranks the responses from most effective to least. Elimination removes the hazard, for example by building the caisson above the water or assembling large parts on the ground. Substitution replaces the hazard with a safer method, such as lifting prefabricated sections instead of riveting them at height. Engineering controls change the workplace itself, such as safety nets under the deck, pipe cooling for concrete, and decompression stages for caisson workers. Administrative controls set rules for work, such as shift limits, training, and inspection schedules. Personal protective equipment, such as hard hats, harnesses, and respirators, is the last line of defense, and it works only when the higher controls are in place.

## History

The Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883, and the Forth Bridge opened in 1890. The Eiffel Tower was completed in 1889, and the Golden Gate Bridge opened in 1937. The Hoover Dam was completed in 1936. The Three Gorges Dam was completed in 2006. The Akashi Kaikyo Bridge opened in 1998, and the Burj Khalifa opened in 2010. Each project depended on the steel, concrete, and surveying methods of its time, and each one tested the limits of those methods.

## Lessons for engineers today

Wind is a design load for any long, light structure. The Tacoma Narrows failure taught engineers to test deck shapes in wind tunnels and to check for flutter. Large projects also carry social costs, such as displaced families and damaged ecosystems, that must be planned from the start. Construction safety depends on the order of the controls: the best protection removes the hazard, and personal equipment comes last.

## Practice

1. A suspension cable has $w = 40\ \text{kN/m}$, $L = 500\ \text{m}$, and $h = 50\ \text{m}$. Find the horizontal tension $H$ in MN.
2. For the same cable load, the span doubles and the sag doubles. Find the new horizontal tension as a multiple of the answer to question 1.
3. Using $w = 40\ \text{kN/m}$ and $L = 500\ \text{m}$, find the vertical component $wL/2$ at the tower in MN.
4. Using the answers to questions 1 and 3, find the tension at the tower top $T$ in MN.
5. Explain in two or three sentences why a suspension bridge with a shallow, light deck is at risk from wind, and name the kind of failure involved.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. $H = 40\,000 \times 500^2 / (8 \times 50) = 25\ \text{MN}$.
2. $H \propto L^2 / h$, so doubling $L$ and $h$ gives $2^2 / 2 = 2$ times the original value, which is 50 MN.
3. $wL/2 = 40\,000 \times 500 / 2 = 10\ \text{MN}$.
4. $T = \sqrt{25^2 + 10^2} = \sqrt{725} = 26.9\ \text{MN}$.
5. A shallow, light deck has low torsional stiffness, so the wind can drive a twisting motion that grows as the structure feeds energy back from the wind. This is aeroelastic torsional flutter, the failure mode of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940.

## Common mistakes

- The Tacoma Narrows Bridge failed because of vortex lock-in alone. Its collapse came from aeroelastic torsional flutter, a self-excited twisting motion in which the deck and the wind interact.
- The Eiffel Tower was built only as a temporary structure. It was granted a permit for 20 years, and its survival came from radio antennas that gave it a lasting use.
- Dams hold back water only by their weight. Gravity dams do use weight, but arch dams transfer load to the canyon walls, so the shape matters as much as the mass.
- The Forth Bridge is repainted in one endless cycle. Corrosion control is an ongoing task, but the painting work is organized in cycles of inspection and repair.
- The Hoover Dam's concrete cured quickly. Its large mass gave off heat, and pipe cooling was needed to keep the concrete from cracking as it set.
