# Ancient monuments and waterworks

**Type:** lesson

From about 2600 BCE to the early centuries CE, builders in Egypt, Rome, Iran, Cambodia, and China solved problems of stone, water, and scale that engineers still meet. This lesson covers six projects from that span and the engineering each one needed. The aqueduct gradient example uses the same open channel flow ideas as the fluid systems lesson.

**Prerequisites:** [Simple machines](../01-simple-machines.md), [Ancient engineering](../02-ancient-engineering.md), [Fluid systems](../../applications/thermal-fluid/02-fluid-systems.md)
**Time:** 4-6 h

## Core ideas

### The Great Pyramid of Giza

The problem. Khufu, a pharaoh of Egypt's Fourth Dynasty, needed a tomb that would last and that would show his power. The builders had no iron tools, no wheels for heavy loads, and no lifting machines beyond ramps, levers, and ropes.

The engineering. Crews cut limestone from quarries near the site and granite from Aswan, hundreds of kilometres to the south. They moved blocks on sledges. A tomb painting and later experiments suggest that wetting the sand under a sledge cut the friction. Boats carried stone along the Nile and a canal to the plateau. The base was leveled to within a few centimetres, and the sides face the cardinal directions to a small fraction of a degree.

The scale. The pyramid was about 146 m tall when new, with a base about 230 m on each side. It contains more than two million stone blocks. It was the tallest human-made structure on Earth for nearly four thousand years.

What went wrong. The outer casing of smooth white limestone was later stripped away and reused in buildings in Cairo, which left the stepped core visible today. Herodotus, writing about two thousand years after the pyramid was built, described a workforce of enslaved people. Excavations of workers' villages, bakeries, and cemeteries near Giza point instead to organized crews, some of which received medical care after injuries. The Wadi al-Jarf papyri, found in 2013, include the logbook of an official named Merer, which records boats that carried limestone to Giza.

How it changed the world. The pyramid shows what a state can organize at scale. It needed quarrying, surveying, transport, food supply, and accounts for thousands of workers over about twenty years. Modern large projects still depend on those same kinds of planning.

### Roman aqueducts and the Pantheon

The problem. Rome grew into a very large city, and wells and the Tiber could not supply enough clean water. The city needed water brought from distant springs.

The engineering. Roman engineers moved water by gravity alone. An aqueduct channel fell gently, usually a few metres per kilometre, so the flow stayed steady. The channel was lined with waterproof mortar. Settling tanks let sediment drop out before the water reached the city. Where a valley was too deep to follow, the channel crossed on a row of arches. Where a valley was narrow and the water had to drop and rise again, builders used an inverted siphon, a pipe under pressure that ran down into the valley and back up. Concrete made of lime, volcanic ash, and rubble filled the arches and vaults.

The scale. The Aqua Appia, begun in 312 BCE by Appius Claudius Caecus, was the first Roman aqueduct, and most of it ran underground. Rome had eleven aqueducts by the early third century CE. The Pont du Gard in southern France carries an aqueduct over a river valley. It is about 49 m high and about 275 m long at the top, and it has three tiers of arches. Hadrian's Pantheon in Rome, completed about 125 CE, has a concrete dome about 43 m across with an open oculus near 9 m across at the top.

What went wrong. Water theft was common. Sextus Julius Frontinus, who was the water commissioner for Rome around 97 CE, wrote a report that names illegal connections and wasted water. In 537 CE, during the Gothic War, besiegers cut several aqueducts, and the defenders had to repair them. Much Roman building relied on enslaved and hired labor, and the surviving records of work crews are thin.

How it changed the world. Reliable water let cities grow dense, with public baths, fountains, and sanitation. The Pantheon's dome stayed the largest of its kind for about 1,300 years, until Brunelleschi's dome in Florence was completed in 1436. The Roman concrete mix and its arch and vault forms set a pattern that later builders copied.

### The Great Wall of China

The problem. Northern Chinese states needed to defend their borders against mounted raiders, control movement along the frontier, and send signals over long distances.

The engineering. Builders used rammed earth, stone, and brick, depending on what was nearby. They placed watchtowers at intervals so that signal fires could carry news along the line. Walls were built in phases by several dynasties. The Qin unified China in 221 BCE and joined earlier walls into one line. Later Han walls reached west to Dunhuang. The Ming walls, built from 1368 to 1644, are the brick and stone sections that most visitors see today.

The scale. The walls of all periods together run for many thousands of kilometres across the north of China.

What went wrong. The Qin and later rulers used forced labor to build the walls. Many workers died, but no reliable total exists. The walls did not decide the fate of the Ming capital. In 1644 Beijing fell after Wu Sangui opened the Shanhai Pass, at the eastern end of the wall, to Manchu forces.

How it changed the world. The wall shaped the border, trade, and control of movement for two thousand years. It also became a national symbol in modern times, and UNESCO has listed sections as a World Heritage Site.

### The Grand Canal of China

The problem. Grain grown in the wet, rice-producing south had to reach the capital in the north. Rivers in China flow mostly east to west, so the route had to link them with canals that climb and descend.

The engineering. Builders joined existing canals and rivers into one waterway. Pound locks, which raise or lower boats between gated chambers, let the canal climb between water levels. Engineers dug new channels where needed and built embankments to hold the water and protect the fields.

The scale. The canal is about 1,800 km long at its main route. The Sui dynasty completed the main work about 609 CE. In the 13th century, under the Yuan dynasty, the route was straightened and moved toward Beijing.

What went wrong. Labor drafts and costly military campaigns weighed heavily on the Sui court, which fell in 618 CE, less than four decades after it was founded in 581. The people who dug the canal paid a large share of the cost in forced labor, and the survivors' records are sparse.

How it changed the world. The canal linked the north and the south economically and politically, and parts of it still carry boats today. UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List in 2014.

### Qanats of Iran

The problem. Iran is dry, and many towns sit on plains where rain is scarce and surface water evaporates quickly. Open channels lose water to the sun and wind, and pumping water from deep wells needs energy that early societies did not have.

The engineering. A qanat is a gently sloping tunnel that carries groundwater from a source at the foot of hills to the settlement or fields below. Vertical shafts placed at intervals let workers dig and clean the tunnel. The water flows by gravity, and it stays underground where evaporation is small. The tunnel drops only a small amount over its length, so the water moves slowly and steadily.

The scale. Qanats have been in use for more than two thousand years, and some networks run many kilometres. UNESCO inscribed the Persian qanat system as a World Heritage Site in 2016.

What went wrong. Shafts collapsed, and tunnels flooded or dried out as the water table shifted. In modern times, deep pumping lowered the water table so far that many qanats stopped flowing. Diggers worked in narrow, dark tunnels and faced cave-ins and bad air.

How it changed the world. The qanat lets a dry region support towns, gardens, and farms without surface reservoirs or pumps. The same design spread to North Africa and other dry regions over the centuries.

### Angkor's water system

The problem. Monsoon rain falls heavily in the wet season and very little falls in the dry season. Rice needs water in both, and the capital of the Khmer Empire needed a steady supply for a large population.

The engineering. Builders constructed large earthen reservoirs called barays, along with canals, dikes, and sluices. The West Baray is about 8 km long and about 2 km wide. Embankments held the water, and channels carried it to fields and temples. Tanks and moats stored water near the monuments.

The scale. The temple complex and its water system were built between about the ninth and twelfth centuries. Airborne laser scanning has since mapped a larger network of roads, canals, and reservoirs than earlier maps showed.

What went wrong. Angkor declined during the 15th century. Scholars debate the roles of drought, flooding, war, and failing water control. The system needed constant maintenance, and when water control broke down, fields and cities suffered.

How it changed the world. The baray system supported rice farming and a dense population for centuries. It is one of the clearest examples of large-scale water management in premodern Asia.

## Key equations

The Manning equation estimates the mean velocity in an open channel that flows under gravity:

$$V = \frac{1}{n} R^{2/3} S^{1/2}$$

where $V$ is the mean velocity in metres per second (m/s), $n$ is the Manning roughness coefficient in s/m$^{1/3}$ (a dimensionless value for many use cases, typically about 0.013 for finished masonry or concrete), $R = A/P$ is the hydraulic radius in metres (m), $A$ is the wetted cross-section area in square metres (m$^2$), $P$ is the wetted perimeter in metres (m), and $S$ is the channel slope, a dimensionless ratio of drop to length.

The flow rate follows from continuity:

$$Q = A V$$

where $Q$ is the volume flow rate in cubic metres per second (m$^3$/s).

The slope comes from the total drop and the channel length:

$$S = \frac{\Delta z}{L}$$

where $\Delta z$ is the fall in metres (m) and $L$ is the channel length in metres (m).

## Worked example

**Given:** A Roman-style channel runs $L = 50\ \text{km}$ and drops $\Delta z = 17\ \text{m}$ over that length. Assumptions: the channel is rectangular, $0.6\ \text{m}$ wide with water $1.0\ \text{m}$ deep, and it is lined with masonry with a typical roughness $n = 0.013$.

**Find:** the gradient, the mean velocity, and the flow rate.

**Solution:**

1. Gradient: $S = 17 / 50\,000 = 3.4 \times 10^{-4}$, which is about 1 in 2940, or 0.34 m of fall per km.
2. Area: $A = 0.6 \times 1.0 = 0.60\ \text{m}^2$.
3. Wetted perimeter: $P = 0.6 + 2(1.0) = 2.6\ \text{m}$.
4. Hydraulic radius: $R = A/P = 0.231\ \text{m}$.
5. Velocity: $V = \frac{1}{0.013} (0.231)^{2/3} (3.4 \times 10^{-4})^{1/2} = 0.534\ \text{m/s}$.
6. Flow rate: $Q = 0.60 \times 0.534 = 0.320\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$, which is about $2.8 \times 10^4\ \text{m}^3$ per day.

**Answer:** $S = 3.4 \times 10^{-4}$ (about 1 in 2940). $V = 0.53\ \text{m/s}$. $Q = 0.32\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$.

**Sanity check:** A gradient of 1 in 3000 is very gentle, so a velocity of about half a metre per second is plausible for a channel of this size. A flow of 0.32 m$^3$/s is 320 litres per second, a useful supply for a town. Doubling the depth would not double the flow, because the hydraulic radius and the wetted area change together. Real channels also lost water through leaks and sediment, so the delivered flow was below the calculated value.

## Design trade-offs

A gravity aqueduct trades construction effort for running cost. A steep channel moves water fast, but it needs more drops, more tanks, or faster erosion control. A gentle channel needs no pumps, but it must be very long, and it must run in a straight line or on arches. Builders chose arches for valleys and siphons for deep dips, and each choice added cost in masonry or pipe. Sediment settling and cleaning access add more trade-offs, because a channel that is not cleaned silts up and loses capacity.

## How it fails

Aqueducts fail when water leaks through cracked mortar, when channels silt up, or when a valley crossing loses its support. Siphons fail when pressure exceeds the pipe strength. Qanats fail when shafts collapse or when the water table drops below the tunnel. Reservoirs fail when embankments wash out, and the Angkor system depended on maintaining embankments and sluices through each monsoon. Sabotage and war also cut water supplies, as in Rome in 537 CE.

## History

Early Roman aqueducts began with the Aqua Appia in 312 BCE. Later aqueducts reached Rome from farther away, and the Pont du Gard was built in southern France during the first century CE. Engineers in Iran developed the qanat over more than two thousand years, and Angkor's reservoirs were built during the Khmer period. Each project used the tools of its time, including surveying levels, ropes, levers, ramps, and mortar.

## Lessons for engineers today

Gravity moves water over long distances if the slope is controlled. The aqueduct and qanat designs still inform gravity water systems today. Maintenance is part of the design. Roman aqueducts had commissioners, cleaning crews, and rules against illegal taps, and qanats needed regular digging to stay open. Large projects need planning, records, and labor management as much as they need technical skill.

## Common mistakes

- The pyramids were built by enslaved people. Archaeology at Giza points to organized, paid crews. The enslaved-labor account comes from Herodotus, centuries after construction.
- The Great Wall is visible from space with the naked eye. Astronauts have reported that they cannot see it without aid, and the wall is narrow compared with the landscape around it.
- Roman aqueducts carried water uphill. They relied on a continuous fall, and the inverted siphon was used only where a valley forced the pipe down and back up with enough pressure to push the water over the rise.
- Doubling the slope does not double the velocity. Velocity scales with the square root of the slope, so a fourfold steeper channel gives only about twice the velocity.
- The Pantheon dome had steel reinforcement. It was built from concrete with a lighter aggregate near the top, and it had no steel bars.
