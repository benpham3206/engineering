# Lubrication and tribology

**Type:** lesson
**Verify:** strict

Tribology is the study of surfaces in relative motion: their friction, wear, and lubrication. It matters because friction wastes power as heat and wear ends the life of nearly every moving machine part, so a fluid film a few micrometres thick often decides whether a bearing or gear lasts a day or a decade. This lesson explains how surfaces touch, how they wear, how a lubricant film forms and carries load, and how to choose a lubricant for bearings and gears.

**Prerequisites:** [Shafts and bearings](08-shafts-and-bearings.md), [Control volumes and pipe flow](../physics/11-control-volumes-pipe-flow.md), [Mechanical properties](../materials/03-mechanical-properties.md)
**Time:** 6-8 h

## Core ideas

### Friction

**Friction** is the force that resists sliding between two surfaces in contact. **Coulomb friction** is the model that the friction force is proportional to the normal force, $F = \mu N$, with $\mu$ the **coefficient of friction**, independent of the apparent contact area and of sliding speed. It is a first model that works for dry metals and fails for lubricated contact, where the force depends on speed and film.

Why $\mu N$ and not area: real surfaces are rough, and touch only at tips of their roughness, called **asperities**. The **real area of contact** is far smaller than the apparent area. The asperities deform until their total area carries the load, so that area is the load divided by the hardness of the softer material. The friction force is the shear strength of those junctions times the same real area, so the load cancels out of the area and leaves a ratio of shear strength to hardness that does not depend on the apparent area.

Static friction (before sliding starts) is usually larger than kinetic friction (once sliding). The difference causes stick-slip, the juddering of a violin string or a squealing brake.

### Wear mechanisms

**Wear** is the progressive loss of material from a surface by sliding, rolling, or impact. Four mechanisms account for most of it.

- **Adhesive wear.** Asperity junctions weld and then tear, moving material from one surface to the other. It is the main wear when clean metals slide dry, and it ends in seizure or galling.
- **Abrasive wear.** Hard asperities or hard particles cut or plough a softer surface. It is the worst offender in contaminated machines. A hard particle between two surfaces scratches both, so filtering and sealing the lubricant protects a bearing more than choosing a better one.
- **Surface fatigue wear.** Repeated stress cycles, as in rolling bearings and gear teeth, crack the subsurface and release flakes (pitting or spalling). It depends on contact stress and number of cycles and is covered with bearing life in [Shafts and bearings](08-shafts-and-bearings.md).
- **Corrosive wear.** Chemical attack forms a layer, such as oxide, that rubs away, and the fresh surface corrodes again. Fretting, the small oscillating motion in a clamped joint, wears this way and is why preloaded joints in [Bolted and welded joints](12-bolted-and-welded-joints.md) must not slip.

### Lubrication regimes

A **lubricant** is a substance placed between surfaces to reduce friction and wear. Its job is to replace solid contact with a film that shears easily. The **lubrication regime** is how much of the load is carried by the film and how much by asperities.

- **Boundary lubrication.** The film is only a few molecules thick, and asperities touch. Friction and wear are set by chemical films on the surfaces, built up from additives, not by viscosity. The coefficient of friction is relatively high.
- **Mixed lubrication.** The film carries part of the load and asperities carry the rest.
- **Hydrodynamic lubrication.** A full fluid film separates the surfaces. The film pressure is generated by the motion itself: the moving surface drags oil into a converging wedge, and the pressure rises to carry the load. Friction comes only from shearing the oil, so it is low and there is no wear in steady operation. It needs a minimum speed to build the film.
- **Elastohydrodynamic lubrication (EHL).** At small, highly loaded contacts, such as gear teeth and rolling bearing balls, the pressure is so high that the surfaces flatten elastically and the oil viscosity rises sharply with pressure. A thin film (a fraction of a micrometre to a micrometre) then survives. NASA's report, [Elastohydrodynamic Lubrication: Status of Understanding](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19840016980), gives an overview.

The **Stribeck curve** plots the friction coefficient against the dimensionless group $\mu_o N / P$ (viscosity times speed over load per area, defined below). At very low values, boundary lubrication gives a high, flat friction. As the group rises, the film grows, friction falls to a minimum where mixed lubrication ends and full film begins, and then rises slowly as film shear grows. Start-up and shut-down of a bearing pass through the left side of this curve, which is why wear concentrates there.

The **specific film thickness** $\lambda$ compares the film to the roughness: $\lambda = h_\text{min}/\sigma$, with $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}$ the combined root-mean-square roughness of the two surfaces. Typical rule of thumb: $\lambda < 1$ is boundary, $1$ to $3$ is mixed, and above about $3$ is full film. These limits are typical, not exact.

### Viscosity

**Dynamic viscosity** ($\mu_o$, SI unit Pa s) is the shear stress needed per unit velocity gradient. A fluid between a plate moving at speed $U$ and a fixed plate a gap $h$ away carries a shear stress $\tau = \mu_o U / h$ if the velocity varies linearly (simple Couette flow). **Kinematic viscosity** ($\nu$, SI unit $\text{m}^2$/s) is $\mu_o/\rho$, which sets how a fluid flows under its own weight. The common unit centistoke is 1 $\text{mm}^2$/s.

Viscosity falls steeply as an oil warms. A two-constant model that fits mineral oils well over a limited range is $\mu_o = A \exp(B/T)$, with $T$ in kelvin (an Andrade form, valid for narrow ranges). A bearing that runs hotter than designed has a thinner oil film, and a small increase in temperature moves the bearing left on the Stribeck curve. Viscosity also rises with pressure, which is what makes EHL work.

### Journal bearings and the Sommerfeld number

A **journal bearing** is a cylindrical sleeve around a rotating shaft (the **journal**), with a small radial clearance $c$ filled with oil. At rest the shaft sits at the bottom, touching. As it turns it drags oil into the wedge between itself and the sleeve and lifts itself up on a film. The shaft then runs off-centre by an **eccentricity** $\epsilon c$.

The **Sommerfeld number** $S$ is the dimensionless group that sets the operating state of the bearing:

$$
S = \frac{\mu_o N}{P}\left(\frac{r}{c}\right)^2
$$

A large $S$ (high viscosity, high speed, light load) means a thick film and a shaft close to the centre. A small $S$ means a heavy load, a thin film, and a shaft near the sleeve, where the film is thinnest and the bearing is closest to contact. For a given geometry, the bearing's friction, film thickness, and flow follow from $S$ and the length-to-diameter ratio, usually from charts or tables in design handbooks, or from the Reynolds equation solved numerically.

**Petroff's equation** is the friction law for a lightly loaded journal bearing where the shaft is assumed to run concentric. It is a hand estimate of friction, and is exact only at zero load. It shows why friction scales with the same group $S$.

### Greases and choosing a lubricant

**Grease** is a lubricating oil held in a thickener (often a metal soap), plus additives. It stays where it is put, seals out dirt, and needs no pump, but it carries less heat away than a circulating oil and gives a thicker film at low speed.

- **Rolling bearings.** Grease suits low to moderate speed, sealed-for-life bearings, and vertical shafts. Oil suits high speed, high temperature, or when heat must be carried away, because oil can be cooled and filtered. In a rolling bearing, the oil film is EHL, so the oil viscosity at operating temperature, not the grease type alone, sets the film. A grease is classified by consistency (how stiff it is) and by the thickener and base oil.
- **Journal bearings.** Oil is used almost always, by splash, ring, or pressure feed. Choose the viscosity so the operating $S$ gives enough film at the highest expected temperature.
- **Gears.** Gear teeth roll and slide in EHL contact. Higher load and slower speed need higher viscosity, and heavily loaded gears with sliding (worm and hypoid gears) need extreme-pressure additives that form sacrificial films at the contact. At high pitch-line speed, oil is circulated to cool the gears and churning loss rises, so a thinner oil is used.
- **Vacuum, space, and extreme temperature.** Liquid lubricants evaporate or freeze. Dry film lubricants such as molybdenum disulfide and graphite, and low-vapour-pressure oils and greases, are used instead.

## Key equations

Symbols: $F$ = friction force (N), $N_\text{n}$ = normal force (N), $\mu$ = coefficient of friction (dimensionless), $\mu_o$ = dynamic viscosity (Pa s), $U$ = sliding speed (m/s), $h$ = gap (m), $\rho$ = density ($\text{kg/m}^3$), $N$ = shaft speed (rev/s, which is 1/s for these formulas), $W$ = bearing load (N), $L$ = bearing length (m), $D$ = journal diameter (m), $r$ = journal radius (m), $c$ = radial clearance (m), $P$ = projected pressure (Pa).

Coulomb friction (dry contact, a first model):

$$
F = \mu\, N_\text{n}
$$

Newton's law of viscosity for a linear velocity profile:

$$
\tau = \mu_o \frac{U}{h}, \qquad \nu = \frac{\mu_o}{\rho}
$$

Viscosity against temperature (Andrade form, over a limited range), with $A$ and $B$ fitted from two measurements at $T_1$ and $T_2$:

$$
\mu_o = A\, e^{B/T}, \qquad B = \frac{\ln(\mu_{o,1}/\mu_{o,2})}{1/T_1 - 1/T_2}
$$

Specific film thickness:

$$
\lambda = \frac{h_\text{min}}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}}
$$

Journal bearing projected pressure and Sommerfeld number:

$$
P = \frac{W}{L D}, \qquad S = \frac{\mu_o N}{P}\left(\frac{r}{c}\right)^2
$$

Petroff's friction torque $T_f$ and coefficient of friction $f = T_f/(W r)$ for a concentric shaft:

$$
T_f = \frac{4\pi^2 \mu_o N r^3 L}{c}, \qquad f = 2\pi^2 \frac{\mu_o N}{P}\frac{r}{c}
$$

The friction power is $T_f\, \omega$, with $\omega = 2\pi N$ in rad/s.

Holds when: the film is full (hydrodynamic), the oil is Newtonian at one temperature, and, for Petroff, the load is light so the shaft is nearly concentric. Breaks when: the bearing is heavily loaded and runs eccentric, the oil heats noticeably through the film, the shaft starts or stops (mixed regime), or the contact is EHL (the pressure raises viscosity).

> [!WARNING]
> A bearing that depends on a hydrodynamic film has no film at rest. If load is applied before the shaft turns, or the oil supply fails, the surfaces touch and can seize. Interlock the load to oil pressure on critical machines, and keep the oil clean and cool.

## Worked example

**Journal bearing, Petroff estimate.** A shaft of diameter 50 mm turns at 1800 rpm in a plain bearing 50 mm long with a radial clearance of 0.050 mm. The bearing load is 2.00 kN and the oil has dynamic viscosity 0.040 Pa s at operating temperature. Find the Sommerfeld number, the friction coefficient, and the friction power loss.

**Given:** $D = 50$ mm, $L = 50$ mm, $c = 0.050$ mm, speed 1800 rpm, $W = 2.00$ kN, $\mu_o = 0.040$ Pa s.

**Find:** $S$, $f$, and the power loss.

**Assumptions:** full hydrodynamic film; the oil is Newtonian at the stated temperature; the load is light enough for Petroff's concentric approximation; the stated viscosity is the film's mean viscosity.

**Solution:**

1. Speed: $N = 1800/60 = 30$ rev/s. Radius: $r = 25$ mm.
2. Projected pressure: $P = W/(L D) = 2000/(0.050 \times 0.050) = 8.0 \times 10^5$ Pa.
3. Radius to clearance ratio: $r/c = 25/0.050 = 500$.
4. Sommerfeld number: $S = (0.040 \times 30/8.0\times10^5)(500)^2 = 1.5\times10^{-6} \times 250\,000 = 0.375$.
5. Friction coefficient: $f = 2\pi^2 \times 1.5\times10^{-6} \times 500 = 0.0148$.
6. Friction torque: $T_f = f W r = 0.0148 \times 2000 \times 0.025 = 0.74$ N m.
7. Power: $\omega = 2\pi \times 30 = 188.5$ rad/s, so the loss is $0.74 \times 188.5 = 140$ W.

**Answer:** $S = 0.375$, $f = 0.0148$, and the friction power loss is about 140 W, which the oil carries away as heat.

**Sanity check:** the coefficient 0.0148 is about one tenth of a dry sliding value of 0.15 or more, as expected for a fluid film. As a cross-check, the Petroff torque formula $4\pi^2 \mu_o N r^3 L / c$ gives the same torque.

<details><summary>Verification</summary>

```verify
D = q(50, "mm")
L = q(50, "mm")
c = q(0.050, "mm")
N = q(1800, "") / q(60, "s")
W = q(2.00, "kN")
mu = q(0.040, "Pa s")
given("2", "0.15", "0.0148", "0.74", "8.0", "1.5", "250000")
expect(N, "30 /s")
r = D / 2
expect(r, "25 mm")
expect(r, "0.025 m")
expect(W, "2000 N")
P = W / (L * D)
expect(P, "8.0e5 Pa")
expect(r / c, "500")
S = (mu * N / P) * (r / c)**2
expect(mu * N / P, "1.5e-6")
expect(S, "0.375")
f = 2 * pi**2 * (mu * N / P) * (r / c)
expect(f, "0.0148")
Tf = f * W * r
expect(Tf, "0.74 N m")
Tf2 = 4 * pi**2 * mu * N * r**3 * L / c
expect(Tf2, "0.74 N m")
w = 2 * pi * N
expect(w, "188.5 /s")
expect(Tf * w, "140 W")
check(f < 0.1, "friction coefficient is well below dry sliding values")
```

</details>

**Viscosity at a different temperature.** A mineral oil has $\mu_o = 0.060$ Pa s at 40 C and $0.0085$ Pa s at 100 C (typical values for a medium-grade oil, stated inputs for this example). Find its viscosity at 70 C with the Andrade model.

**Solution:**

1. Convert to kelvin: $T_1 = 313.15$ K, $T_2 = 373.15$ K, $T_3 = 343.15$ K.
2. $B = \ln(0.060/0.0085)/(1/313.15 - 1/373.15) = 3806$ K.
3. $A = 0.060/\exp(3806/313.15) = 3.16 \times 10^{-7}$ Pa s.
4. At 70 C: $\mu_o = A \exp(3806/343.15) = 0.0207$ Pa s.

**Answer:** about 0.0207 Pa s, which is one third of the viscosity at 40 C. Between 40 C and 100 C the viscosity drops by a factor of 7.06.

**Sanity check:** the 70 C value lies between the two measurements, and the logarithm of the viscosity is a straight line in $1/T$, as the model requires.

<details><summary>Verification</summary>

```verify
m1 = q(0.060, "Pa s")
m2 = q(0.0085, "Pa s")
T0 = const("t_celsius_zero")
T1 = T0 + q(40, "K")
T2 = T0 + q(100, "K")
T3 = T0 + q(70, "K")
given("0.060", "0.0085", "100", "70", "40")
expect(T1, "313.15 K")
expect(T2, "373.15 K")
expect(T3, "343.15 K")
B = log(m1 / m2) / (1 / T1 - 1 / T2)
expect(B, "3806 K")
A = m1 / exp(B / T1)
expect(A, "3.16e-7 Pa s")
m3 = A * exp(B / T3)
expect(m3, "0.0207 Pa s")
expect(m1 / m2, "7.06")
check(m3 < m1 and m3 > m2, "70 C value lies between the measurements")
```

</details>

## Practice

1. A steel block pressed with 500 N slides on a dry steel plate with $\mu = 0.15$. Find the friction force in N.
2. An oil has dynamic viscosity 0.060 Pa s and density 870 $\text{kg/m}^3$. Find its kinematic viscosity in $\text{mm}^2$/s (centistokes).
3. Two plates 0.10 mm apart have oil of viscosity 0.050 Pa s between them. One moves at 2.0 m/s relative to the other. Find the shear stress in kPa.
4. A bearing film has minimum thickness 0.80 micrometres. The surfaces have root-mean-square roughness 0.40 and 0.30 micrometres. Find $\lambda$ and state the regime.
5. A bearing's oil warms so that its viscosity halves, while the speed is doubled and the load is unchanged. Find the ratio of the new Sommerfeld number to the old, and say what this implies for the friction coefficient from Petroff's equation.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. 75 N
2. 69.0 $\text{mm}^2$/s
3. 1.0 kPa
4. $\lambda = 1.6$, the mixed regime (between 1 and 3).
5. The ratio is 1.00. Petroff's friction coefficient depends on $\mu_o N/P$, which is unchanged, so $f$ is unchanged at the same $r/c$.

```verify
mu = q(0.15, "")
expect(mu * q(500, "N"), "75 N", ans=1)
nu = q(0.060, "Pa s") / q(870, "kg/m^3")
expect(nu, "69.0 mm^2/s", ans=2)
tau = q(0.050, "Pa s") * q(2.0, "m/s") / q(0.10, "mm")
expect(tau, "1.0 kPa", ans=3)
lam = q(0.80, "um") / sqrt(q(0.40, "um")**2 + q(0.30, "um")**2)
expect(lam, "1.6", ans=4)
check(lam > 1 and lam < 3, "mixed regime")
expect(q(0.5, "") * q(2, ""), "1.00", ans=5)
```

</details>

## Common mistakes

- Applying $F = \mu N$ with a dry coefficient to a lubricated contact. The friction of a fluid film depends on viscosity, speed, and load, not on a constant $\mu$.
- Using the viscosity at the supply temperature. The film is hotter, and the viscosity at film temperature, not the reservoir temperature, sets the result.
- Mixing units for viscosity (Pa s, centipoise, centistoke) and confusing dynamic with kinematic viscosity. Centipoise is $10^{-3}$ Pa s; centistoke is 1 $\text{mm}^2$/s.
- Using speed in rpm in $S$. The formula needs rev/s, so that the group is dimensionless.
- Treating Petroff's friction as a design value for a loaded bearing. It assumes a concentric shaft. A loaded shaft runs eccentric, which raises friction and lowers the minimum film.
