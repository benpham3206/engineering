# Shop safety

Shop safety is the first skill in manufacturing because every other skill depends on it. A machine that can cut metal can also cut a hand, pull in a sleeve, or throw a chip into an eye. An engineer who designs parts also needs to know how they get made safely.

**Prerequisites:** none
**Time:** 3-5 h

## Core ideas

- **Hazard**: a source of harm, such as a spinning spindle or a hot weld.
- **Risk**: the chance that a hazard hurts someone, combined with how bad the harm could be.
- **Control**: anything that lowers risk. Controls work best in this order: remove the hazard, guard it, add warnings, then rely on personal protective equipment (PPE).
- **Lockout/tagout (LOTO)**: a set of steps that makes a machine unable to start while someone works on it. The energy source is switched off, locked, tagged, and checked with a test before work begins.
- **PPE**: gear worn on the body, such as safety glasses, hearing protection, and gloves. PPE is the last line of defense, not the first.

Most shop injuries come from a small set of causes:

- Rotating parts that grab loose sleeves, gloves, hair, or rags.
- Pinch points where two moving parts meet, such as belts and pulleys or press tools.
- Flying chips, broken tools, and parts that come loose.
- Stored energy: springs, hydraulic pressure, raised weights, and charged capacitors.
- Noise, fumes, dust, and hot surfaces that cause slow damage you do not feel at once.

Guards are fixed barriers or interlocked covers that keep hands and parts out of the danger zone. A guard you remove "for a quick look" is a broken control. Interlocks stop a machine when a guard opens. Emergency stops must be reachable without reaching into the hazard.

Noise is a good example of a slow hazard. OSHA sets an action level of 85 decibels averaged over an 8-hour day, above which a hearing conservation program is required. Hearing loss does not hurt, so people skip protection. Use earplugs whenever the machine is running.

## Checklist

Before you run any machine, check these points:

- Read the machine manual and the shop rules for that machine.
- Confirm you have been trained on it. Do not operate a machine you were not shown.
- Wear safety glasses with side shields at all times in the shop.
- Remove rings, watches, and loose jewelry. Tie back long hair. Do not wear gloves near rotating parts.
- Roll up or tuck in loose sleeves.
- Check that all guards are in place and that interlocks work.
- Know where the emergency stop is and test that it works before you start.
- Clamp the workpiece. A part that moves is a part that can fly.
- Clear the work area of tools, rags, and chips before you start the machine.
- Never reach past a running tool to grab a chip. Stop the machine, then clear it with a brush or hook.
- Apply LOTO before you clean, adjust, change a tool, or repair anything inside the guard.
- Report near misses. A near miss is a free warning.

## Example

**Given:** A bench drill press with a 1/2 in (12.7 mm) drill bit. The operator plans to drill a steel bracket held by hand.

**Find:** The hazards and the controls to use before drilling.

**Assumptions:** The press has a guard over the belt and pulleys and an on/off switch within reach.

**Solution:**

1. Hazard: the bit can grab the bracket and spin it. A hand-held part can wrap around the operator's hand. Control: clamp the bracket to the press table with a vise or clamp.
2. Hazard: the rotating chuck and bit can catch sleeves or hair. Control: no gloves, tie back hair, roll up sleeves.
3. Hazard: chips can fly into the eyes. Control: safety glasses with side shields.
4. Hazard: a drill that jams can twist the operator. Control: keep both feet planted, use a steady feed, and keep one hand on the feed lever.
5. Hazard: the belt and pulleys pinch. Control: keep the guard closed. Never adjust belts or speed with the machine running.

**Answer:** Clamp the bracket, wear glasses and no gloves, keep the belt guard closed, and use a steady feed. Check that the emergency stop is reachable.

**Sanity check:** Every hazard in the list has a control that does not depend on the operator being careful. Clamping and guards work even when someone is tired.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting PPE alone. Glasses do not stop a spinning part from hitting your hand.
- Removing a guard "just for a moment" and forgetting to replace it.
- Reaching into a running machine to grab a chip or measure a part.
- Skipping LOTO because the job seems quick. Most serious accidents happen during short tasks.
- Thinking hearing damage and fume exposure do not count because you do not feel them at once.
