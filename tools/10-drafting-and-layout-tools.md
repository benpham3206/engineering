# Drafting and layout tools

**Type:** lesson

Drafting and layout tools are the hand instruments that engineers and machinists have used for centuries to put lines, arcs, and angles on paper and on metal. CAD does most of this work today, but a sketch on the back of an envelope or a scribed line on a steel bar still matters when a computer is not nearby, when a part must be marked by hand, or when you need to check a CAD result with your own eyes.

**Prerequisites:** [Measurement tools](07-measurement-tools.md) for reading rules, calipers, and tolerances. [Engineering graphics](../design/02-engineering-graphics.md) for views, line types, and drawing conventions. Basic trigonometry from [the math folder](../math/README.md).
**Time:** 8-12 h to learn the tools, plus regular practice at a bench or drafting table

## Core ideas

This lesson is a practical guide. For each tool it covers what the tool is for, how to choose it, and how to use it without error. The drawing rules themselves (views, line types, dimensioning) are in the engineering graphics lesson linked above. Reading rules and calipers are in the measurement lesson.

Two rules apply to every tool here:

- Keep the tool clean and undamaged. A nicked straightedge or a dull pencil point ruins every line drawn with it.
- Draw light first, then darken. Construction lines are thin and light. Final lines are darker. Erase the construction lines when the drawing is final.

### Pencils, leads, and erasers

Drafting pencils and leads are graded by hardness. A harder lead (H, 2H, 3H, and so on) leaves a lighter, finer line and wears to a point more slowly. A softer lead (B, 2B, and so on) leaves a darker line and smudges more.

- Use hard leads (2H to 4H) for construction lines and layout. They are easy to erase.
- Use an H or F lead for normal visible lines.
- Use HB for lettering and notes.
- Use a mechanical pencil with a 0.5 mm or 0.7 mm lead for consistent width. Keep the lead tip sharp with a sharpener or a lead pointer.

Erasers each have a job:

- A vinyl eraser removes graphite cleanly and leaves little residue. It is the everyday choice.
- A kneaded eraser lifts light graphite without rubbing the paper. Use it on construction lines.
- An eraser shield guides the eraser over a single area so you do not erase neighboring lines.

Do not press hard and do not scrub. Hard erasing thins the paper and leaves a shine that shows up in later prints.

### Steel rules and scales

A steel rule is a straight, rigid bar with marks on one or both edges. It is the basic measuring and straight-line tool. Choose one with clear, fine marks and an edge that is straight and undamaged.

A **scale** is a special ruler where one unit on the scale stands for a larger or smaller length on the real object. Scales let you draw a large part on a small sheet. Three types are common:

- **Architect's scale.** Marks are in feet and inches. The common scale 1/4 in = 1 ft means that every quarter inch on paper stands for one foot of real length. Other common scales are 1/8 in = 1 ft and 3/8 in = 1 ft.
- **Engineer's scale.** Marks are in decimal inches. A scale marked 10 has ten divisions per inch, and a scale marked 40 has forty. Use it for mechanical parts that are dimensioned in decimal inches.
- **Metric scale.** Marks are in millimetres. The common reduction scales are 1:2, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:50, and 1:100. A full-size scale is 1:1.

Read a scale with care:

- A ratio scale such as 1:2 means the drawing is one part in two parts real size. A line 84 mm long on the part is drawn 42 mm long on paper.
- The scale that reads 1:10 gives real lengths directly when you use its marked edge. Check the label on the edge before you read a value.
- Use the scale's own edge. Do not use the end of the scale as a zero point unless the scale has a clear zero mark there. Many scales start a short distance from the end.

Steel rules are also used alone for straight lines and for checking lengths. Hold the rule on the surface and draw against the edge with the pencil leaning slightly away from the direction of travel.

### Straightedges, T-squares, and triangles

A straightedge guides a pencil or scriber along a straight line. Check the edge against a known straight surface before you trust it.

A **T-square** is a straightedge with a crosspiece, or head, that slides along the side of a drawing board. Its blade is used for horizontal lines. Keep the head pressed firmly against the board edge.

Two drafting triangles are standard:

- A **30-60-90 triangle** has angles of 30, 60, and 90 degrees. Its sides are in the ratio 1 : $\sqrt{3}$ : 2. Use it for 30, 60, and 90 degree lines and for vertical lines.
- A **45-45-90 triangle** has two equal angles of 45 degrees and a right angle. Use it for 45 degree lines and 90 degree corners.

An **adjustable triangle** pivots around a fixed corner so that you can set any angle. It is handy for angles that the fixed triangles do not give, such as 15 or 75 degrees.

Lines drawn with a T-square and triangles are parallel when you slide the triangle along the blade. Always draw from the left to the right on a horizontal line, and hold the triangle firmly so it does not slip.

### Protractors and bevel protractors

A **protractor** is a half circle or full circle with angle marks. Place the protractor center on the vertex of the angle and align one side of the angle with the zero line. Read the mark where the other side crosses the scale. Read it twice, once from each direction, to avoid reading the wrong scale on a double-scale protractor.

A **bevel protractor** has two arms that pivot. Set one arm on a reference surface and the other on the angled surface. It reads the angle between the two arms and is used to copy an angle from a part to a drawing or back again.

### Compass and dividers

A **compass** holds a pencil or lead at a fixed radius to draw circles and arcs. Set the radius on a steel rule, not by eye. Keep the needle point in the center and hold the compass by its top, leaning slightly in the direction of travel.

**Dividers** have two sharp points and no pencil. They step off equal distances, transfer a length from one place to another, and check spacing. To step off a distance, set the points to the length on a scale and walk them along a line. Count the steps.

Five constructions are common and worth practicing until they are automatic:

- **Drawing an arc or circle.** Set the compass to the radius, place the needle at the center, and rotate the compass.
- **Bisecting a line.** Set the compass to more than half the line length. Draw an arc from each end, on both sides of the line. The two arcs cross at two points. The line through those two points is the perpendicular bisector, and it cuts the line into two equal halves.
- **Bisecting an angle.** Place the needle at the vertex and draw an arc that crosses both sides of the angle. Then draw two arcs from those crossing points, with the same radius, so they cross inside the angle. A line from the vertex through that crossing point bisects the angle.
- **Dividing a line into equal parts.** Draw the line. From one end, draw a second line at any small angle. Use dividers to step off equal spaces along the second line, for example 5 spaces. Join the last step to the other end of the original line. Draw parallel lines from each step to the original line with a triangle. The original line is now divided into equal parts.
- **Constructing a regular hexagon.** Draw a circle with radius R. Without changing the compass radius, step it around the circle. Six steps land on six points, which are the corners of a regular hexagon. In a regular hexagon the side length equals the circle radius.

### Circle and ellipse templates, and French curves

A **circle template** is a plastic sheet with holes of many standard diameters. It draws circles faster than a compass for common sizes.

An **ellipse template** has ellipses at several axis ratios. Use it for circles seen at an angle, such as holes drawn in an isometric view. Check the template's axis ratio against the view before you trace.

A **French curve** is a plastic curve with many arcs of different radii. Bend a curve through a few points, then trace the smooth part that fits. Trace a short section, move the curve so that the next part matches, and continue. A French curve gives a smooth, curved line that a compass cannot draw.

### Combination square set

A combination square has a steel blade with a graduated scale and a head that slides along the blade and locks with a thumb screw. Three heads are common:

- **Square head.** It has a 90 degree face and a 45 degree face. Use it to check that a surface is square to another, and to scribe lines parallel to an edge.
- **Center head.** It has two faces that meet at 90 degrees, and the 45 degree lines cross at the center of the blade. Place it on the end of a round bar and scribe a line across the end. The line is a diameter.
- **Protractor head.** It has a swivel scale that reads angles from 0 to 180 degrees, or a bevel reading. Use it to set and check angles on metal.

Check the square head against a known flat surface and against the blade. A square that is out by a few tenths of a degree will make parts that do not fit.

## Layout on metal

Layout is the work of marking exact lines and points on a part before cutting, drilling, or milling it. Good layout starts with a flat, true reference and uses the same datum for every mark.

- **Surface plate.** A flat, heavy plate of granite or cast iron. It is the reference surface for all layout work on metal. Keep it clean and cover it when not in use.
- **Layout fluid.** A thin coat of dye or blue fluid on the metal. It makes a scribed line easy to see. Let it dry before scribing. Wipe off the layout fluid after use.
- **Scriber.** A hardened steel point that scribes a fine line in the layout fluid. Lean it about 15 degrees in the direction of travel and pull it along a straightedge or square. Keep the point sharp.
- **Height gauge.** A vertical scale on a base with a sliding head that carries a scriber. Set the height on the scale, then scribe a line on the part at that height. Measure heights from the surface plate, not from the table.
- **Center punch.** A punch with a wide point, about 90 degrees, that makes a small dimple to start a drill. Strike it once, firmly, on the exact point.
- **Prick punch.** A punch with a fine point, about 30 degrees, that marks a precise point on a scribed line. Use it where the mark must be exact, such as at the intersection of two lines. Use a center punch to enlarge the dimple once the prick punch has marked the point.
- **Dividers on metal.** Set the dividers to a radius from a steel rule, then scribe arcs in the layout fluid. The point of the divider leaves a sharp mark for drilling.
- **V-blocks.** Blocks with a V-shaped groove that hold round stock steady. Place the round stock in the V, and use a height gauge or square to scribe on it. Use a pair of V-blocks for long bars.

### Finding the center of a round bar

Place the bar in a V-block on the surface plate and butt it against a square to set it level. Use a center head combination square on the end of the bar. Scribe a line across the end face with the center head. This line is a diameter. Rotate the bar 90 degrees, or to any other angle, and scribe a second line across the end. The two lines cross at the center of the bar. Use a prick punch to mark the center.

Check the center by repeating the process with a third line. All three lines should pass through the same point. If they form a small triangle, the center is in the middle of the triangle, and you should recheck the bar and the square.

## Hand sketching for design

A sketch is a fast drawing used to think, not to dimension. Engineers sketch to explore shape, check proportion, and show an idea to a teammate. A sketch that is not to scale is still useful if the proportions are right.

### Proportion

- Lay out the overall box first. Find the long and short sides, and sketch them in the correct ratio.
- Use a known length as a reference. For example, if a handle must be about one-third the length of the body, draw the body first and place the handle by eye.
- Check each feature against the box. Ask whether it is larger or smaller than the expected fraction.

### Isometric sketching

An isometric sketch shows three faces of a part at once. Draw three axes from one point: a vertical line, and two lines at 30 degrees from horizontal, one to the left and one to the right. Draw the edges of the object along these three directions. Edges that are not parallel to an axis need their endpoints located first, then joined.

A line along an isometric axis is drawn at an angle of 30 degrees, so its horizontal part is shorter than the full line. The horizontal part equals the length times $\cos 30^\circ \approx 0.866$. Use this ratio as a check when you sketch a cube. For a quick sketch, matching proportions by eye is enough.

Use isometric grid paper if you have it. Draw the grid first, and place the object on it.

## Key equations

For $n$ equal holes spaced evenly on a circle of diameter $D$ (in mm), the straight-line distance between two adjacent hole centers, the chord $c$, is:

$$c = D \sin\left(\frac{180^\circ}{n}\right)$$

Here $D$ is the diameter of the circle of hole centers in mm, $n$ is the number of holes, and $c$ is the chord in mm. The formula gives the straight line between neighbors, which is shorter than the arc between them.

The arc length between adjacent holes is:

$$s = \frac{\pi D}{n}$$

with $s$ in mm. Because a chord is always shorter than its arc, $c < s$. Use this as a check.

Scale conversion from a drawing to real size is:

$$L_{\text{real}} = L_{\text{drawing}} \times k$$

where $L_{\text{drawing}}$ is the length on paper, $k$ is the scale factor (for example $k = 2$ for a 1:2 scale), and $L_{\text{real}}$ is the real length. Use the same unit for both lengths.

For isometric sketching, a length $L$ along an isometric axis appears in the sketch with horizontal run:

$$L_{\text{h}} = L \cos 30^\circ$$

## Worked example

**Given:** A round plate has a circle of six equal holes on a bolt circle of diameter $D = 100$ mm. The holes are evenly spaced around the circle.

**Find:** The chord length to step off with dividers to locate each hole center. Also find the arc length between holes as a check.

**Assumptions:** The holes are equally spaced. The dividers are set on a steel rule to the computed chord. The bolt circle center is marked on the plate.

**Solution:** Run the arithmetic in Python.

```python
import math

D = 100.0   # bolt circle diameter, mm
n = 6       # number of holes

chord = D * math.sin(math.radians(180.0 / n))   # chord between adjacent holes, mm
arc = math.pi * D / n                           # arc length between adjacent holes, mm

print("chord =", round(chord, 2), "mm")
print("arc   =", round(arc, 2), "mm")
print("chord < arc:", chord < arc)
```

Output:

```
chord = 50.0 mm
arc   = 52.36 mm
chord < arc: True
```

**Layout steps:**

1. Mark the center of the plate on the surface plate with a scriber and a height gauge. Find the center using two diagonals, as described in the layout section above.
2. Scribe a circle of radius 50 mm around the center with a compass or dividers. The circle is the bolt circle.
3. Set the dividers to 50.0 mm on a steel rule. Check the setting twice.
4. Place one divider point on the circle at any starting point, and step the other point around the circle. Scribe a small mark where each step lands. Six steps bring you back to the start.
5. Use a prick punch on each mark. Check the six marks for equal spacing with the dividers.

**Answer:** The chord between adjacent hole centers is 50.0 mm. Set the dividers to 50.0 mm and step off six times around the 100 mm circle.

**Sanity check:** The chord (50.00 mm) is shorter than the arc (52.36 mm), as expected. Also, for six holes the chord equals the radius, which is the hexagon rule. Because $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$, the chord is $D/2 = 50$ mm exactly.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the wrong scale on a multi-scale rule. Check the label on the edge before you measure.
- Setting dividers by eye instead of on a steel rule. Small setting errors grow over six or twelve steps.
- Stepping off holes around a circle without checking the final point. If the last step does not land on the start, the spacing is wrong.
- Scribing lines on a surface that is not flat, or measuring heights from the table instead of the surface plate.
- Using a worn or bent square. Check it against a known flat surface before you use it to mark critical lines.
