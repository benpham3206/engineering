# Hand and power tools

**Type:** lesson
**Verify:** strict

**Hand and power tools** are the screwdrivers, wrenches, pliers, saws, drills, and grinders that an engineer uses to build, fix, and test real hardware. A designer who has used them draws parts that can be reached, tightened, and cut, and a person who knows how each one injures people or damages parts can work alone without getting hurt.

This lesson starts from the first time you hold a tool: what it is, what its parts do, and the one safe first use. Then it covers the normal uses, and the judgment that separates a careful worker from a lucky one. Machine speeds are in [Shop tools](08-shop-tools.md) and [Machining speeds and feeds](../manufacturing/02-machining-speeds-and-feeds.md); this lesson links the formula there and does not restate it in full.

**Prerequisites:** [Shop tools](08-shop-tools.md), [Shop safety](../manufacturing/01-shop-safety.md), [Units and dimensional analysis](../measurement/01-units-and-dimensional-analysis.md)
**Time:** 4-6 h reading, plus supervised practice with each tool

## Core ideas

### The rules that apply to every tool

- A **hazard** is a source of harm and a **control** lowers the chance of harm; the order of controls is in [Shop safety](../manufacturing/01-shop-safety.md). For hand and power tools the controls that matter most are a clamped workpiece, a guard left in place, the right tool for the job, and eye protection.
- Wear safety glasses for every task in this lesson. Add hearing protection for the saws, grinders, and sanders, and a dust mask or extraction for sanding and cutting.
- No loose sleeves, jewelry, gloves near rotating tools, or hair that can hang into a spinning part. A rotating tool does not stop for cloth; it winds it in.
- Disconnect the power cord or remove the battery before changing a blade, bit, disc, or belt.
- Hold the work in a clamp or vise, never in your hand. If the tool grabs the work, the clamp takes the force and your hand does not.
- Let the tool do the cutting. Pushing harder overloads the tool and makes it jump.

> [!WARNING]
> Every tool here can injure within a second. Do not use a power tool you have not been shown how to use, and follow the shop's own rules where they are stricter than these. A guard that has been removed "for a quick look" no longer protects anyone.

### Screwdrivers and drive types

A **screwdriver** turns a screw by pressing a shaped tip into a recess in the screw head and applying torque. The **drive type** is the shape of that recess and tip.

- A **slotted** drive is a single straight slot. The tip slides out sideways easily, so it suits low torque.
- A **cross recess** drive has two crossed slots with sloped sides. Phillips and Pozidriv are the common versions. They look alike and are not interchangeable; a Phillips tip in a Pozidriv screw fits loosely and slips.
- A **hex socket** drive and a **six-lobe star** drive have flat or rounded walls that are nearly parallel to the turning axis, so they carry high torque with little force pushing the tip out.
- A **square** drive is used on some wood and electrical screws and resists slipping.

**Cam-out** is the tip climbing out of the recess under torque. It happens when the walls of the recess slope: the turning force then has a part that pushes the tip outward along the screw axis. It damages the recess and then the screw cannot be driven or removed. To prevent it, choose the exact drive type and size, press firmly along the screw axis, and turn slowly. A tip that is worn or the wrong size is the most common cause.

> [!NOTE]
> A screwdriver is for turning screws, not for prying, scraping, or punching. A bent or chipped tip should be replaced, since a tip that does not fill the recess will round it.

### Wrenches and sockets

A **wrench** is a lever with a jaw that grips the flats of a nut or bolt head. A **socket** is a cylinder with a hole shaped for the fastener, driven by a handle. The torque on the fastener is the force on the handle times its length from the center of the fastener, $T = F L$.

- An **open-end** wrench slips on from the side but grips on only two flats, so it is for light torque or places a box cannot reach.
- A **box-end** wrench encircles the head. A 6-point box or socket grips the flats along their length; a 12-point version slips on faster but grips corners and rounds a worn head sooner.
- A **combination** wrench has an open end and a box end of the same size.
- A **ratchet** drives a socket in one direction and lets the handle swing back without removing the socket. It is for speed, not for the final tightening or the first breaking loose of a stuck fastener.
- An **adjustable wrench** has a movable jaw. Set the jaw snug on the flats and turn it so the force pushes toward the fixed jaw, never toward the movable one. It is the last choice because it never fits as well as a fixed size.

Pull on a wrench rather than pushing. If it slips while you pull, your hand moves away from the work; if it slips while you push, your knuckles meet the part. Use a longer handle only if the fastener is meant to take it, and never add a pipe over a handle on a small fastener.

### Metric and inch sizes, and why a near fit rounds a fastener

A fastener head is sized by the distance across its flats. Metric heads use millimetres (for example 13 mm), and inch heads use fractions of an inch (for example 1/2 in). Some of the two are nearly the same, so a wrench of the wrong system can seem to fit.

A wrench that is a little too large touches the head only at its corners, not along the flats. The same torque is then carried by a small area at each corner, so the corners deform and the head rounds off. Once the head is round, the wrench slips and the fastener often cannot be removed. A wrench a little too small does not go on at all. The rule is simple: use the wrench or socket that fits snugly with no rocking, and when you meet a head that seems to fit two sizes, test both and choose the one with less play. Compute the play with the worked example below.

Using the wrong system is a design problem as well as a shop problem. A designer who mixes metric and inch fasteners in one assembly forces the user to carry two sets and invites this mistake.

### Pliers, hex keys, knives, and clamps

- **Slip-joint** and **combination** pliers grip and bend small parts. They are not for turning nuts, because the jaws round the flats.
- **Needle-nose** pliers reach into tight places to place or hold small parts and wire.
- **Diagonal** (side) cutters cut wire. Cut only wire the jaws are rated for, and aim the cut-off end away from your eyes.
- **Locking** pliers clamp on with a screw-adjusted lever and stay closed. They hold a part, or grip a rounded fastener as a last resort.
- A **hex key** is an L-shaped bar with a hexagonal section. Insert it fully into the socket before turning. The short arm gives more torque and the long arm gives more reach; a **ball-end** key works at an angle but carries less torque, so tighten with the straight end.
- A **utility knife** has a snap-off or replaceable blade. Cut away from your body with the other hand behind the blade. A sharp blade needs less force and slips less than a dull one. Retract the blade after every use.
- A **clamp** holds work against a surface or another part. A **C-clamp** has a screw; a **bar clamp** slides along a rail; a **spring clamp** is quick but light. Put the clamp pad on a flat area, protect a finished surface with scrap, and do not hang a clamp where the cutter will reach it.

### Power tools

A **power tool** converts electrical or battery power into fast motion at the tip, usually rotation. Fast motion is what makes it useful and dangerous. The three hazards that repeat are **kickback** (the tool or work thrown back at you when the cutter binds), **entanglement** (clothing, hair, or a glove pulled into the rotating part), and **flying debris** (chips, sparks, fragments of a broken blade or disc). Most tools have one of these as the main risk.

#### Cordless drill and impact driver

A **cordless drill** spins a bit steadily. It has a **clutch**, a ring that slips at a set torque so the drill does not over-tighten a screw or twist your wrist. A **chuck** grips the round or hex shank of the bit.

An **impact driver** adds short, rapid rotational blows on top of steady turning, which gives a high peak torque with little reaction felt in the hand. It takes hex-shank bits. Use the drill for holes, for precise driving with the clutch, and for small or soft fasteners. Use the impact driver for long screws and large fasteners that need high torque. Do not use it to drill precise holes or to drive small screws, since the blows strip heads and break small bits.

The main hazard is the work or the drill spinning when the bit binds, especially when it breaks through the far side. Clamp the work, keep a firm grip, and ease off as the bit exits. The habit is to start slowly, keep the bit straight, and use the side handle on a larger drill.

#### Drill press

A **drill press** holds the drill in a fixed column, so the bit moves straight down and the depth can be set. It sets speed by moving a belt between pulley steps or by a dial. The formula for the right speed is in [Shop tools](08-shop-tools.md); see the worked example below.

The hazards are entanglement of loose clothing or hair, and the work spinning free when the bit grabs. Clamp the work to the table or hold it in a vise bolted to the table, never in your hand. Remove the chuck key before starting. Keep the guard in place and use a backing block under the work so the bit does not cut into the table.

#### Band saw

A **band saw** is a continuous blade of thin steel running around two wheels, cutting downward through the work. It cuts curves and stock of many shapes, and the cut is smooth and moves the chips down.

The hazard is the blade cutting fingers close to it, and a blade that breaks. Set the upper blade guide just above the work. Feed the work with a steady, light force and use a push stick near the blade. Do not cut round stock freehand, because it rolls and pulls the work in.

#### Miter saw and circular saw

A **miter saw** swings a circular blade down onto work held against a fence, for accurate crosscuts at set angles. A **circular saw** is a hand-held blade that you push along the work, for long cuts.

Kickback is the main hazard of both, and it is worst on the circular saw. If the kerf, the slot the blade cuts, closes on the blade, or the blade twists out of line, the saw is thrown back toward you. Support the work so the cut-off piece does not pinch the blade, set the blade depth just below the thickness of the work, and keep the guard able to retract. On a miter saw keep both hands clear of the blade path, hold the work against the fence, and let the blade stop before lifting it. The blade keeps coasting after the switch is released.

#### Angle grinder

An **angle grinder** spins a disc at very high speed on the side of its head. With a grinding disc it removes metal; with a cut-off disc it cuts; with a wire wheel it cleans.

Flying fragments are the main hazard: discs can shatter, and sparks and metal chips fly off. Entanglement and kickback follow if the disc snags. Use only a disc whose rated maximum speed is at least the grinder's speed, and always use the guard. Hold the grinder with both hands, keep the side handle on, and stand so that sparks and a broken disc miss you. Use a cut-off disc only for cutting and a grinding disc only for grinding; side loading a thin cut-off disc can break it. Let it reach full speed before touching the work.

#### Belt and disc sanders

A **belt sander** moves an abrasive belt, and a **disc sander** spins an abrasive disc, to shape or smooth a part. The work is pressed against the moving surface.

The hazard is the pinch point between the abrasive and the table, which can draw fingers in, and the heat and dust. Keep the table gap small, feed the work against the direction that pulls it down onto the table, and stay clear of the pinch point. Dust from metals and plastics is an inhalation hazard, and sparks from steel can ignite dust, so use extraction and keep sparks away from stored dust.

#### Rotary tool

A **rotary tool** is a small high-speed tool that holds tiny cutters, sanding drums, and thin discs. It reaches detail work that a larger tool cannot.

The main hazard is a thin disc that shatters at its very high speed, and a tool that jumps when the cutter catches. Wear a face shield with safety glasses for cutting, use a disc only as the manufacturer permits, and hold the work in a clamp.

#### Heat gun

A **heat gun** blows air heated to several hundred degrees Celsius for shrinking tubing, softening plastic, and loosening adhesive. It is not a hair dryer.

The hazards are burns, fire, and fumes from heated coatings and plastics. Keep the nozzle moving, away from flammable material, and out of reach of anything you cannot see behind the work. Let it cool, resting on its stand, before you put it down. Work with ventilation, and do not heat an old coating of unknown content.

| Tool | Main hazard | Guard or habit that prevents it |
|---|---|---|
| Cordless drill, impact driver | Bit binds and the tool twists | Clamp the work, hold firmly, ease off at breakthrough |
| Drill press | Entanglement; work spins | Clamp the work, remove the chuck key, keep clothing and hair clear |
| Band saw | Cut fingers; blade breaks | Guide just above the work, push stick, never freehand round stock |
| Miter saw, circular saw | Kickback; blade coasts | Support the offcut, blade depth just through, wait for the blade to stop |
| Angle grinder | Disc bursts; sparks | Rated disc, guard on, both hands, stand out of the line of the disc |
| Belt and disc sanders | Pinch point; dust | Small table gap, feed the work onto the table, extract dust |
| Rotary tool | Thin disc shatters | Face shield, clamped work, discs used as rated |
| Heat gun | Burns, fire, fumes | Keep the nozzle moving, away from flammables, ventilate |

## Key equations

Surface speed and spindle speed for a drill of diameter $D$ turning at $N$ (the SI form of the formula in [Machining speeds and feeds](../manufacturing/02-machining-speeds-and-feeds.md)):

$$
V = \pi D N, \qquad N = \frac{V}{\pi D}
$$

Here $V$ is the surface speed at the outer edge of the cutter (m/s, but quoted in m/min), $D$ is the cutter diameter (m), and $N$ is the spindle speed in revolutions per second (quoted in rev/min, or rpm).

Torque on a fastener from a force on a handle:

$$
T = F L
$$

Here $T$ is the torque (N m), $F$ is the force perpendicular to the handle (N), and $L$ is the distance from the center of the fastener to where the force acts (m).

Play between a wrench and a head, for a wrench opening $w$ and flats-to-flats size $s$:

$$
g = w - s
$$

Here $g$ is the gap (mm), $w$ is the wrench opening (mm), and $s$ is the head size across flats (mm). A negative value means the wrench is too small to go on.

## Worked example

### Drill speed from a surface speed target

A 10 mm drill bit will cut mild steel. The supplier gives a surface speed target of 30 m/min for this bit and material. The drill press has a belt with these spindle speeds available: 620 rpm, 1100 rpm.

**Given:** $D = 10$ mm, $V = 30$ m/min, available speeds 620 rpm and 1100 rpm.

**Find:** The ideal spindle speed, the speed to choose from the two available, and the surface speed that choice gives.

**Assumptions:** The target 30 m/min is an input from the supplier, not a recommendation of this lesson. The bit is sharp, and the work is clamped.

**Solution:**

1. Convert $V$ and $D$ to SI: $V = 0.5$ m/s and $D = 0.01$ m.
2. $N = V/(\pi D) = 15.9$ rev/s, which is 955 rev/min.
3. The press has no 955 rpm setting. Choose the lower step, 620 rpm, because a speed above the target heats the cutting edge faster than a speed below it.
4. At 620 rpm the surface speed is $V = \pi D N = 19.5$ m/min.

**Answer:** The ideal speed is 955 rpm. Use 620 rpm, which gives 19.5 m/min.

**Sanity check:** 1100 rpm would give 34.6 m/min, above the target, so 620 rpm is the nearer step below. In inch units the target is about 98 ft/min.

<details><summary>Verification</summary>

```verify
V = q(30, "m/min")
D = q(10, "mm")
expect(V, "0.5 m/s")
expect(D, "0.01 m")
N = V / (pi * D)
expect(N, "15.9 1/s")
expect(N, "955 1/min")
N1 = q(620, "1/min")
N2 = q(1100, "1/min")
V1 = pi * D * N1
expect(V1, "19.5 m/min")
V2 = pi * D * N2
expect(V2, "34.6 m/min")
check(V1 < V, "620 rpm is below the target")
check(V2 > V, "1100 rpm is above the target")
expect(V, "98 ft/min")
```

</details>

### Wrench fit for near sizes

A drawer holds metric wrenches of 8, 11, 13, 14, and 19 mm. A set of bolts has inch heads of 0.3125, 0.4375, 0.5, 0.5625, and 0.75 in across the flats.

**Given:** $s$ in inches, $w$ in millimetres.

**Find:** The gap $g$ for each metric wrench on its nearest inch head, and which pair rounds a head or does not fit.

**Assumptions:** The wrench opening equals its nominal size, and the head is exactly its nominal size.

**Solution:** Convert each inch size to millimetres, then subtract.

| Metric wrench | Inch head | Head size | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 mm | 0.3125 in | 7.94 mm | 0.06 mm |
| 11 mm | 0.4375 in | 11.11 mm | -0.11 mm |
| 13 mm | 0.5 in | 12.70 mm | 0.30 mm |
| 14 mm | 0.5625 in | 14.29 mm | -0.29 mm |
| 19 mm | 0.75 in | 19.05 mm | -0.05 mm |

**Answer:** The 11, 14, and 19 mm wrenches are smaller than the inch heads and do not go on. The 8 mm wrench on a 0.3125 in head leaves 0.06 mm of play, which is small enough to seem to fit and turn the corners. The 13 mm wrench on a 0.5 in head leaves 0.30 mm, which is enough to round the head under high torque.

**Sanity check:** The gap is positive only when the wrench is larger than the head. The play is under half a millimetre in every pair, which is why the mistake is easy to make by eye.

<details><summary>Verification</summary>

```verify
a = q(0.3125, "in") 
expect(a, "7.94 mm")
expect(q(8, "mm") - a, "0.06 mm")
b = q(0.4375, "in")
expect(b, "11.11 mm")
expect(q(11, "mm") - b, "-0.11 mm")
c = q(0.5, "in")
expect(c, "12.70 mm")
expect(q(13, "mm") - c, "0.30 mm")
d = q(0.5625, "in")
expect(d, "14.29 mm")
expect(q(14, "mm") - d, "-0.29 mm")
e = q(0.75, "in")
expect(e, "19.05 mm")
expect(q(19, "mm") - e, "-0.05 mm")
check(q(13, "mm") > c, "13 mm is larger than 0.5 in")
```

</details>

## Practice

1. A 6 mm drill bit has a surface speed target of 25 m/min. Find the spindle speed in rpm.
2. A 3/8 in (0.375 in) drill bit has a surface speed target of 100 ft/min. Find the spindle speed in rpm, to the nearest whole number.
3. A 14 mm wrench is tried on a 0.5625 in head. Find the gap in mm to two decimals, and say whether the wrench fits.
4. A 10 mm hole must be drilled in a tough alloy with a surface speed target of 12 m/min, but the lowest speed the drill press has is 620 rpm. Find the speed the target requires, the surface speed at 620 rpm, and name one way to proceed.
5. You must put a 60 mm screw into a hardwood beam and then drill a 3 mm pilot hole for it in a thin aluminium bracket. Name the tool for each job and why.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. 1326 rpm
2. 1019 rpm
3. -0.29 mm. The wrench is smaller than the head and does not fit.
4. The target requires 382 rpm. At 620 rpm the surface speed is 19.5 m/min, above the target, so the edge overheats and dulls. Options are a drill with a lower speed range, a smaller step with a pilot hole, or a different press speed range.
5. The long screw needs high torque, so use an impact driver (after a pilot hole for the wood). The 3 mm pilot hole in thin aluminium needs control, so use a drill with a clutch or a drill press, with the bracket clamped, since an impact driver's blows can break the small bit.

```verify
D6 = q(6, "mm")
V6 = q(25, "m/min")
expect(V6 / (pi * D6), "1326 1/min", ans=1)
Din = q(0.375, "in")
Vf = q(100, "ft/min")
expect(Vf / (pi * Din), "1019 1/min", ans=2)
gap = q(14, "mm") - q(0.5625, "in")
expect(gap, "-0.29 mm", ans=3)
check(gap < q(0, "mm"), "the wrench is smaller than the head", ans=3)
D10 = q(10, "mm")
Vt = q(12, "m/min")
expect(Vt / (pi * D10), "382 1/min", ans=4)
expect(pi * D10 * q(620, "1/min"), "19.5 m/min", ans=4)
skip(ans=5, why="tool choice")
```

</details>

## Common mistakes

- Using a wrench that almost fits. Test for rocking, and choose the size with the least play.
- Using pliers or an adjustable wrench as the first choice on a nut that a fixed wrench or socket fits.
- Pushing a wrench away from you, or pulling it toward a sharp part.
- Holding small work in the hand while drilling, sanding, or grinding, instead of in a clamp or vise.
- Leaving the chuck key in a drill chuck, or the guard off a grinder or saw.
- Running a drill at the same speed for every material and bit size. The surface speed, not the rpm, is what the material wants.
- Using an impact driver where control matters, or a drill where high torque is needed.
- Changing a blade, disc, or bit with the tool still plugged in or the battery in.
