# Teardowns

**Type:** lesson

A teardown is taking a product apart in a planned, careful way so you can see how it is built. Engineers do teardowns to learn how a competitor's product is put together, to repair something, or to study a design they are about to improve. A good teardown records every part, how each part is held, and what order the parts came off in, so you can put the product back together or rebuild it on paper.

**Prerequisites:** none
**Time:** 4-6 h

## Core ideas

- A teardown has three goals: understand the structure, record it well enough to rebuild it, and do it without hurting yourself or damaging evidence.
- Before you touch anything, read the label, look for a service manual, and search for any warning about batteries, springs, or capacitors.
- Photograph the product from every side before you open it. Photograph each step as you go. Photos keep details you would otherwise forget.
- Work in the order you remove things. Take the outer shell off first, then the next layer, and so on. Reversing the order later is easier when you know the sequence.
- Keep fasteners sorted. Use a tray or a labeled cup for each group, and note where each fastener came from. A screw that looks the same as another may have a different length or thread.
- Stored energy is the main hazard. Springs under tension, charged capacitors, lithium batteries, and parts held under load can release energy suddenly. Discharge capacitors and remove batteries before you cut or pry anything.
- Lockout means making sure a machine cannot start or move while you work on it. The idea is the same for a household appliance: unplug it, and keep the plug where you can see it.
- An **exploded parts list** is a list of every part with a number, a name, a quantity, and a short note on how it fits. It is named after an exploded view drawing, where parts are drawn pulled apart along their assembly axis.
- A **parting line** is the seam where two halves of a mold met. It shows up on the finished part as a thin line or ridge. You will use parting lines again in the material and process lesson.

## Checklist

Use this before you start any teardown.

- [ ] Product identified: make, model, serial number, and date code recorded.
- [ ] Power source isolated. Battery removed if the product has one.
- [ ] Stored energy checked: springs, capacitors, gas cylinders, and anything under tension.
- [ ] Work surface clean, with a tray for fasteners and a bag for small parts.
- [ ] Safety glasses on. Cutting tools used only where the case is not sealed around a hazard.
- [ ] Full set of photos taken before any part is removed.
- [ ] Teardown log started (see the template below).
- [ ] Plan for reassembly: you know how you will verify the product works again.

## Example

A small desk lamp has a clamp base, a flexible arm, and a plastic head. The owner wants to know if the head can be replaced with a different shade.

1. Unplug the lamp and record the make and model from the sticker on the base.
2. Photograph the lamp from four sides and the head from the front and back.
3. Remove the two screws in the base cover. Photograph the open base, then log both screws as type M3 length about 10 mm, quantity 2, from step 3.
4. Look for a spring in the arm. The arm holds its shape by friction, so no stored spring energy was found. Note this in the log.
5. Undo the head's clip housing. Log each clip and its direction of release. The clips release toward the arm, so the head comes off along the arm axis.
6. Write the exploded parts list. There are 11 parts in total: base, cover, two screws, arm tube, cable, socket, two clips, head shell, shade, and one gasket.

Result: the head is held by two clips and one gasket, with no hidden screws. A new shade can be fitted if its clip position matches.

## Practice

1. A product has 4 screws, 2 clips, and 1 gasket. How many fasteners in total, counting screws and clips but not the gasket?
2. You take photos of 6 sides of a box and then 9 steps inside. How many photos did you take in total if each side and each step gets one photo?
3. An exploded parts list has 12 numbered parts. You find 3 more parts inside a sub-assembly and add them to the list. How many numbered parts are on the list now?
4. A battery pack holds 6 cells. You remove 2 cells before cutting. How many cells remain in the pack?
5. A teardown log has 10 steps, and you skipped 1 step with no photo. How many steps have a photo?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. 6
2. 15
3. 15
4. 4
5. 9

## Common mistakes

- Starting to pry before taking photos. Once a part is off, the position it held is lost.
- Mixing fasteners from different locations in one tray. Two screws of the same look can have different lengths.
- Forgetting stored energy. A spring or charged capacitor can release suddenly.
- Writing only "removed screws" instead of the type, length, and location. Vague notes make rebuilding guesswork.
- Skipping the exploded parts list. The list is what turns a pile of parts into a design you can discuss.
