# Global and social impact

**Type:** philosophy

Engineering changes how people live, work, travel, get water and power, and communicate, and it does so unevenly across regions and social groups. Engineers care because the people who gain or lose from a project are often not the people who make the design choices.

**Prerequisites:** [What engineering is](01-what-engineering-is.md), [Ethics and professional responsibility](04-ethics-and-professional-responsibility.md)
**Time:** 3-5 h

## Core ideas

- Every project has stakeholders: the people who use it, the people who live near it, the people who pay for it, the people who maintain it, and the people it displaces or affects in other ways.
- Impact is not only the intended result. A new road can bring jobs and also noise, pollution, and displacement. A cheap water pump can help a village and also create a maintenance problem if no one can repair it.
- Access matters. A technology that only works with reliable power, a particular language, or expensive parts may leave out the people who need it most.
- Engineers often make decisions about who benefits. Choices about where a plant is built, which routes a road takes, or what standard a product meets all distribute benefits and burdens.
- A useful habit is to ask who is included in the design process. If the answer is only the designer and the client, the design is missing information.
- The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) are a shared framework of 17 goals for global progress. Several bear directly on engineering: clean water and sanitation (goal 6), affordable energy (goal 7), industry, innovation and infrastructure (goal 9), sustainable cities and communities (goal 11), and climate action (goal 13). The full list is on the official UN site.
- Context changes the right answer. A solution that fits a dense city may not fit a rural area. Engineers must learn the local conditions, including climate, culture, economy, and maintenance capacity.

### Engineering in its social context

- Infrastructure shapes opportunity. Roads, schools, clinics, and networks determine how easily people reach jobs, education, and health care.
- Decisions about where infrastructure goes can reinforce or reduce inequality. A highway built through a neighborhood can separate it from jobs and services.
- Technology adoption depends on trust. People use systems they understand and believe will work. Transparency about risks and limits builds that trust.
- Engineers have a role in public debate. Explaining trade-offs in plain language helps communities make informed decisions.

## Checklist

Use this list before you finalize any project with broad effects:

- [ ] Who are all the people affected, including those who did not ask for the project?
- [ ] Who benefits, and who bears the costs or risks?
- [ ] Who will operate and maintain the system after it is built, and do they have the skills and money?
- [ ] Does the design work in the local climate, power supply, language, and economy?
- [ ] Were affected communities consulted, and how?
- [ ] Which SDG or public goal does this project support, and does it trade off against another?
- [ ] What would a failure mean for the people least able to respond to it?

## Example

A town of 5,000 people needs a clean water supply. The old system is contaminated during heavy rain, and households boil water when they can.

- Stakeholders: households, the local clinic, school, farmers who use water for irrigation, the utility staff who will run the system, and downstream communities.
- Options: a new treatment plant on the river, a well field with simple treatment, or a combination with rainwater collection for dry months.
- Impacts: the treatment plant is effective but needs a steady supply of chemicals and trained operators. The well field is cheaper to run but may lower the water table and affect nearby farms.
- Social analysis: the utility can afford the well field, but it has no certified operators. The engineer proposes a simple treatment plant with automated controls, a training program for two local operators, and monitoring that the town can afford.
- Trade-off: the plan costs more up front but depends on local skills and a clear maintenance budget. It is recorded in the public report so residents can question the choice.

The engineer's job is not only to make the water clean. It is to make a system that the town can keep clean for years, using the people and money it actually has.

## Practice

1. Name three stakeholders of a new highway through a city.
   - Key points: a good answer names groups such as nearby residents, drivers, transit riders, businesses, and maintenance crews, and gives a reason each is affected.
2. Give an example where a technical success could still produce a social failure.
   - Key points: the system works but no one can maintain it, the system displaces a group, the system is unaffordable, or it excludes users who lack a required resource.
3. Pick one SDG and explain how an engineering project could support it, and one way it could work against another goal.
   - Key points: a clear link (for example, goal 6 with water treatment), plus a real trade-off (for example, a dam that supports energy and harms river ecosystems).
4. Why should engineers ask who was not consulted in a project?
   - Key points: missing voices often mean missing information about needs, risks, and local conditions; consultation improves the design and builds trust.

Answers are key points a good answer covers. See each question above.

1. Named groups with reasons, such as residents, drivers, transit riders, businesses.
2. Maintenance, displacement, cost, or access failures despite technical success.
3. A clear link to an SDG and a real trade-off with another goal.
4. Missing voices mean missing information; consultation improves design and trust.

## Common mistakes

- Designing for an average user. Real users differ in income, ability, language, and location, and the design must work for the range of them.
- Counting only the benefits a project promises. Look for the costs and the people who pay them.
- Ignoring operations and maintenance. A system with no plan for upkeep fails slowly.
- Assuming a solution from one place will transfer directly to another without checking local conditions.
- Treating public consultation as a formality. Consultation should change the design when it reveals real needs or risks.
