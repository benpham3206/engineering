# Ethics and professional responsibility

**Type:** philosophy

Engineering ethics is the set of duties an engineer owes to the public, employers, clients, and the profession. Engineers care because their work can affect many people who never see the design, and because professional licensing gives them authority that carries responsibility.

**Prerequisites:** [What engineering is](01-what-engineering-is.md), [Safety and risk](03-safety-and-risk.md)
**Time:** 4-6 h

## Core ideas

- Ethics is about what you should do when your duties pull in different directions. Engineering ethics asks how to balance the safety of the public with the wishes of a boss or client, the schedule, and the budget.
- Most professions with licenses have a code of ethics. The NSPE Code of Ethics for Engineers, from the National Society of Professional Engineers in the United States, is one well-known example. In my own words, its core ideas are:
  - Put the safety, health, and welfare of the public first, above the wishes of employers and clients.
  - Work only in areas where you have the training and experience to do the work well, and say so when you do not.
  - Tell the truth in public statements, and give objective, honest reports.
  - Act as a faithful agent or trustee for employers and clients, and protect their confidential information, but never at the cost of public safety.
  - Avoid deception, conflicts of interest, and improper gifts or payments that affect your judgment.
  - Keep your own conduct honest and professional, so the profession keeps public trust.
- Professional duty is not optional for licensed engineers. A licensed engineer who signs a design takes personal responsibility for it.
- Ethical problems often come with pressure: a deadline, a cost target, a manager who wants a different answer. Ethics is practiced in those moments, not in the abstract.
- The usual steps when a concern arises:
  - Identify the facts and who could be harmed.
  - Identify the duties involved, especially the duty to public safety.
  - Consider the options, including raising the issue internally, escalating, or refusing to sign.
  - Document what you found and what you did.
  - Act on the duty to public safety, even if the choice is costly.

### Case: Space Shuttle Challenger (1986)

On January 28, 1986, the Space Shuttle Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch. All seven crew members died. The Presidential Commission (the Rogers Commission) investigated.

What the commission's report found:

- The physical cause was the failure of a joint on the right solid rocket booster. Rubber seals (O-rings) that were meant to block hot gases did not seal properly, and the joint leaked.
- The seals were less resilient in the cold. The launch took place in unusually cold weather.
- Engineers at the contractor had raised concerns about the joint and the cold before launch. The commission criticized the decision-making process that went ahead with the launch despite those concerns.
- The lesson for ethics: a technical concern is only useful if it reaches the people who make the decision, and the people who decide must weigh public safety above schedule and pressure.

### Case: Boeing 737 MAX (2018-2019)

Two Boeing 737 MAX airliners crashed in 2018 (Lion Air Flight 610, in Indonesia) and 2019 (Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302, in Ethiopia). A total of 346 people died.

Accident investigations and later congressional and regulatory reviews found that:

- A flight control function called MCAS (Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System) could push the nose down based on data from one angle-of-attack sensor.
- The system's behavior and its role were not fully described to pilots, and the design was certified with less scrutiny than the aircraft needed.
- Information about the system did not always flow clearly between the manufacturer, the regulator, and the airline.

The ethical question is not only the software. It is also about what the design team knew, what they disclosed, and whether the review processes were allowed to do their job.

## Checklist

Use this list when you face a professional concern:

- [ ] What is the concern, in one sentence, and what evidence supports it?
- [ ] Who could be harmed if the concern is right?
- [ ] Does the concern affect public safety? If yes, that duty comes first.
- [ ] Am I qualified to judge this, or do I need a second expert?
- [ ] Who needs to know, and have I told them in writing?
- [ ] What options do I have if the answer is not acted on? (Escalate, refuse to sign, report to the regulator.)
- [ ] Have I written down the facts and my decisions?

## Example

An engineer reviews a bridge design and finds that the calculated stress at a key connection is close to the allowable limit under a loading case the client has asked the team to drop from the analysis.

- Facts: the loading case is in the applicable design code. The client wants a faster sign-off.
- People at risk: everyone who uses the bridge.
- Duty: the public safety duty comes first.
- Qualification: the engineer is competent in this analysis and should not rely on memory alone.
- Actions: the engineer writes a memo to the project lead with the calculation, the code reference, and a request to include the loading case or get an independent check. If the response is to proceed without the check, the engineer escalates to the firm's technical lead or the licensing board, and refuses to stamp the drawing until the issue is resolved.
- Record: the memo, the reply, and the final decision are kept on file.

The example shows that the duty is not to be right in every argument. It is to make sure the concern reaches people who can act on it, and to refuse to sign work you believe is unsafe.

## Practice

1. In your own words, state the first principle of most engineering codes of ethics.
   - Key points: the public safety, health, and welfare come first, above employer or client wishes.
2. An engineer is told to remove a safety margin to meet a budget. What should they do first?
   - Key points: document the concern, explain the risk in writing, propose alternatives, and escalate if needed; do not silently comply.
3. Why did the Challenger investigation focus on decision-making, not just the seal?
   - Key points: concerns existed before launch, and the process did not weigh them properly; technical risk must reach the decision makers.
4. Why do licensed engineers take on personal responsibility for a signed design?
   - Key points: the license grants authority to the public, and the signature is a claim that the work is competent and safe.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking ethics only applies to dramatic cases. Most ethical choices are small, such as cutting a corner on a test or signing off on a report you have not read.
- Relying on verbal agreements. Write down concerns and decisions so the record is clear.
- Assuming someone else will raise the issue. Many failures involved several people who knew and each assumed another would act.
- Treating the code of ethics as a checklist that ends the discussion. Codes give duties, but the judgment is still yours.
- Confusing loyalty to an employer with obedience. Loyalty is a duty, but it does not override public safety.
