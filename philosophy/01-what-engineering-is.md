# What engineering is

**Type:** philosophy

Engineering is the work of using science, math, and practical judgment to design, build, and maintain things that people rely on. An engineer cares because every design choice affects real people, money, and safety, and the job is to make those choices on purpose instead of by accident.

**Prerequisites:** none
**Time:** 2-4 h

## Core ideas

- Engineering is problem solving under constraints. A problem usually has a goal (move water up a hill, keep a phone cool, carry a load across a river), plus limits on cost, time, materials, space, and the laws of physics.
- Science asks "how does this work?" Engineering asks "how do I make this work for a purpose, within limits, and does it hold up?" Scientists and engineers use the same physics, but an engineer has to commit to a design and answer for it.
- A design is a set of decisions. Each decision has a benefit and a cost. For example, a thicker beam is stronger but heavier and more expensive. Good engineers name the trade-offs and make them visible.
- Engineering is a loop, not a straight line:
  - Define the need and the constraints.
  - Generate options.
  - Model or analyze the options.
  - Build a prototype or test.
  - Measure the results, find the failures, and repeat.
- Engineers work with uncertainty. Material strengths vary, loads change, users do things the designer did not expect. A safe design plans for that instead of assuming everything goes as drawn.
- Engineering is a profession with duties. Licensed engineers in many countries sign their names to designs and can be held responsible for them. That is why ethics and safety are part of the core of the field, not a side topic.

### Main branches

- Civil engineering: roads, bridges, dams, water systems, buildings.
- Mechanical engineering: machines, engines, vehicles, tools, heating and cooling systems.
- Electrical and computer engineering: circuits, power, communication, chips, control systems.
- Chemical engineering: processes that change materials at scale, such as refining fuel or making medicines.
- Aerospace engineering: aircraft and spacecraft.
- Software engineering: programs that run systems, from phones to hospital equipment.

The branches overlap a lot. A modern car uses mechanical, electrical, chemical, and software engineering at the same time.

### What engineers actually do

- Write requirements: what the thing must do, how well, and under what conditions.
- Analyze: calculate loads, heat flow, current, cost, or failure chances.
- Design: choose materials, shapes, parts, and layout.
- Test: check the real thing against the requirements.
- Document: write down the decisions so someone else can understand, build, repair, or check the work later.
- Communicate: explain risk and trade-offs to managers, clients, and the public.

### Engineering versus science, and versus technology

- Science discovers what is true about nature. Engineering applies that knowledge to a goal.
- Technology is the result: the tools, devices, and systems. Engineering is the process that creates them.

## Checklist

Use this list when you start any design problem. It is short on purpose.

- [ ] Who uses this, and what do they need it to do?
- [ ] What must it never do? (The safety requirement.)
- [ ] What are the hard limits? (Cost, size, weight, time, power, materials, law.)
- [ ] What are two or three different ways to meet the need?
- [ ] What does each option cost in each limit?
- [ ] How will I know if the chosen option works? What test would prove it?
- [ ] What happens if a part fails, a user misuses it, or conditions change?
- [ ] Have I written down why I chose this option?

## Example

A town wants a footbridge over a creek. The need is simple: people must cross safely. The constraints are a budget, a span of about 20 m (65 ft), and a flood season.

- Need and constraints: 20 m span, pedestrian loads, a budget, and a 100-year flood level that the deck must sit above.
- Options: a steel truss, a concrete beam, or a timber deck on steel girders.
- Trade-offs: steel is strong and light but needs paint and maintenance. Concrete lasts but is heavy and needs bigger foundations. Timber is cheap and looks good but rots in wet conditions unless treated.
- Choice: a steel girder with a treated timber deck, raised above the flood line. The engineer writes down why, and lists what to inspect each year.
- Check: the engineer asks what happens if a crowd stands on the middle at once, and what happens if a girder corrodes. The answers feed back into the design, such as adding a corrosion coating and checking the load for a crowd.

The example shows the loop at work. The first option is not automatically the final one, and the engineer's written reasons matter as much as the drawing.

## Practice

1. Pick an everyday object, such as a bicycle, a kettle, or a door latch. List the needs it meets and two constraints its designer had to respect.
   - Key points: a good answer names a clear need (for example, "lets a rider go faster"), at least two real constraints (cost, weight, size, safety rules), and at least one trade-off.
2. Explain in your own words why two engineers given the same goal might choose different designs.
   - Key points: different constraints or priorities, different assumptions about the users or loads, different cost and risk tolerance, and both can be valid if the reasons are written down.
3. A design meets all its requirements in a computer model, but the prototype fails in testing. What does this tell you about the process?
   - Key points: models simplify reality, assumptions were wrong or missing, the loop should return to the model with the new data, and testing is part of the design, not a formality.
4. Why do engineers write things down, even when they know the design well?
   - Key points: others must build, check, repair, or reuse the design; memory fails over time; records show why choices were made and support accountability.

1. Need, at least two constraints, and a named trade-off.
2. Different constraints or priorities, different assumptions, and written reasons.
3. Models simplify; assumptions failed; return to the model; testing is part of design.

## Common mistakes

- Treating engineering as only math. Math is a tool. Judgment about trade-offs, safety, and users is equally important.
- Starting with a favorite solution and ignoring the requirements. Define the problem before choosing the answer.
- Assuming the model is the world. A calculation is only as good as its inputs and assumptions.
- Ignoring the users. A design that works in a lab but fails in daily use has not solved the problem.
- Leaving out the failure case. Ask what happens when things go wrong, not only when they go right.
