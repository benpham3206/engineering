# Sustainability

**Type:** philosophy

Sustainability means meeting present needs without damaging the ability of future people to meet theirs. Engineers care because nearly every design uses materials, energy, and land, and it creates waste when it ends its life. Sustainable engineering makes those costs visible and plans for them from the start.

**Prerequisites:** [What engineering is](01-what-engineering-is.md), [Global and social impact](05-global-and-social-impact.md)
**Time:** 3-5 h

## Core ideas

- The most widely used definition of sustainable development comes from the 1987 report "Our Common Future," by the World Commission on Environment and Development (the Brundtland Commission): development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
- Sustainability has three parts that people often discuss together: environmental health, economic viability, and social equity. A design that is green but unaffordable, or cheap but harmful to a community, is not sustainable.
- A system is a stock and flow problem. Resources come into a product as inputs (materials, energy, water), move through it, and leave as outputs (products, emissions, waste). Sustainable design reduces inputs, keeps materials in use longer, and reduces harmful outputs.
- Life cycle thinking looks at the whole life of a product:
  - Raw material extraction and processing.
  - Manufacturing.
  - Transport and distribution.
  - Use, including energy and maintenance.
  - End of life: reuse, recycling, or disposal.
  A product that looks clean in use may have a heavy impact in manufacturing or disposal.
- Durability and repairability are often the largest levers. A product that lasts longer or can be repaired spreads its impact over more years.
- Energy efficiency and material efficiency both matter. Less energy in use reduces emissions over the product's lifetime. Less material per unit of function reduces impact in every stage.
- Engineers face trade-offs. Recycled materials may be harder to make into a specific part. A longer-lasting product may cost more up front. Sustainable engineering names these trade-offs and uses life cycle numbers to compare options.
- Climate and resource limits change the design inputs. Energy prices, water availability, and the local grid carbon intensity all affect which design is best in a given place.

### Principles that come up often

- Reduce, reuse, recycle: prefer using less, then reusing, then recycling materials, in that order of value.
- Design for disassembly: a product that can be taken apart can be repaired, upgraded, or recycled more easily.
- Use local resources when they are sound: transport has its own impact, and local supply reduces it.
- Measure what matters: pick a few metrics you can actually measure, such as energy use, material mass, lifetime, and cost per year of service.

## Checklist

Use this list when comparing two designs for sustainability:

- [ ] What is the function, and what is the simplest way to meet it?
- [ ] Which materials are used, and where do they come from?
- [ ] How much energy does it use in each stage of its life?
- [ ] How long will it last, and can it be repaired or upgraded?
- [ ] What happens at end of life: reuse, recycling, or disposal?
- [ ] Does the cheaper option cost more in the long run once energy and replacement are counted?
- [ ] Who benefits, and who carries the environmental burden?

## Example

Two water heaters both meet the same need: hot water for a household.

- Product A costs \$60 and lasts 2 years before replacement.
- Product B costs \$120 and lasts 6 years.

Compare the cost over a 6-year period. Use only the purchase price for this simple comparison, and assume energy use is the same.

- Product A: cost per year = \$60 / 2 years = \$30 per year. Over 6 years, that is 3 purchases, so the total is \$180.
- Product B: cost per year = \$120 / 6 years = \$20 per year. Over 6 years, that is 1 purchase, so the total is \$120.
- Result: Product B costs about 33 percent less per year of service. It also needs 2 fewer units made, shipped, and eventually discarded over the same 6 years.

Real comparisons add energy use, maintenance, and disposal. The simple example shows the main point: the longer-lasting product spreads its impact across more years. A full life cycle study would check whether Product B's materials or energy use offset that gain.

## Practice

1. Give the Brundtland definition of sustainable development in your own words.
   - Key points: meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
2. Why can a product look environmentally good in use but still have a large overall impact?
   - Key points: the impact in manufacturing, transport, or disposal can be large, so a life cycle view is needed.
3. Using the water heater example, what would change the answer?
   - Key points: a higher energy use for B, a much shorter real life for B, a repair cost that changes the total, or a different disposal impact.
4. Name two design choices that make a product easier to recycle or repair.
   - Key points: design for disassembly, standard fasteners, labeled material types, modular parts, available spare parts.

## Common mistakes

- Looking only at operation. Most of a product's impact can be in its materials or its end of life.
- Treating recycling as a full solution. Recycling still uses energy and often loses material quality, so reducing and extending life come first.
- Comparing products with different functions or lifetimes without adjusting for them.
- Choosing a metric you cannot measure and then claiming a result. Pick measurable numbers and state the uncertainty.
- Ignoring social and economic sustainability. A design that is green but not affordable or accepted will not last.
